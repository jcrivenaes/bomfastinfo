###### **SUMMARY**

This report outlines the work performed in the FEED phase (Front End Engineering Design, phase 7) for the crossing of Bjørnafjorden in the E39 Stord-Os (Hordfast) project, performed by the AMC project group.

The work in this phase has comprised a preliminary design of the chosen base case solution with the goal to optimize the technical solution from earlier phases and reduce the owner’s risk within the project by means of more detailed analyses of the structure and by risk mitigation activities.

The base case solution is an end-anchored floating bridge with supplementary moorings to the seabed, further developed from the K12 alternative in the previous phase of the project. There is also outlined a number of recommendations for further work in order to optimize the solution and to mitigate risk elements towards a possible construction phase.

## **1 Executive summary**

The Norwegian Road Administration (NPRA) has requested planning and engineering services for further development of the Bjørnafjord crossing, consisting of a FEED phase (Front End Engineering Design) as a continuation of phase 5 of the project deducted in 2019.

The companies Aas-Jakobsen, COWI and Multiconsult formed a Joint Venture to perform phase 5, involving the companies Aker Solutions, NGI, Entail, Dissing + Weitling and Moss Maritime as subconsultants (the AMC group). This group was awarded the FEED contract in 2023. The project group is composed of companies with a long history in the Bjørnafjorden project, and key personnel with unique experience from the marine and bridge field, many with a combination of both.

The FEED work started with an initial phase with further development of the preferred technical solution from Phase 5, K12, into an adjusted base case solution for the FEED phase. This base case solution takes into account advantageous details from the various work performed in phase 5 as well as comments to phase 5 solutions outlined by NPRA with external parties. It also takes into account an adjusted design basis made for the FEED phase based on supplementary investigations, code adjustments and updated project specifications.

The base case solution is an end-anchored floating bridge with supplementary side anchors to the seabed with a fixed cable stayed bridge in one end, with a main span suitable for ship passage. The chosen layout is shown in the drawings included in Appendix A of this report.

Throughout this phase of the project, advanced static and dynamic analyses in both frequency domain and time domain have been performed in order to make a thorough design. A risk-based approach has ensured that major risks are identified and sorted out. Risk analyses on operational and construction phases have been used actively in the design process, by means of frequent workshops, to mitigate risk elements.

Main focus areas for the work include:

- Effect of ship collisions on the structure

- Combined wind, wave and current action

- Fatigue design

- Parametric resonance

- Comfort criteria

- Temporary phases

- Anchor locations

- Visual impact

- Robustness

- Risk mitigations

- Cost savings

- Operation and Maintenance

- BIM

The work has produced a relevant basis for a detailed cost estimate and evaluation of the concept.

Conclusively, the base case solution is a robust concept that combines state of the art technology and solutions in a way that will create a world breaking record in bridge construction.

A number of variations to the base case solution is investigated. These are measures that have the potential for cost savings, risk reduction, aesthetical improvement or increase of sustainability. The following variations are recommended for implementation:

- Minimizing overall bridge width by means of adapting a minimum traffic area from governing NPRA handbooks with the final choice of speed limit, also including moving light poles to east side of the bridge.

- Moving supplementary anchors in axis 13 to axis 8 in order to reduce sectional forces and thereby steel quantities and costs in the tower area.

- Adjusting tower layout to account for local optimization of bridge deck support.

## **2 General**

#### **2.1 Introduction**

The Bjørnafjorden bridge is part of the planned road project Hordfast between the mainland city of Bergen and the island of Stord in Vestland county, Norway. Hordfast is part of the "Ferry-free E39" project that aims to improve the European route E39 highway system and link the west coast cities of Kristiansand - Stavanger - Haugesund - Bergen - Trondheim without the use of ferries to cross the numerous fjords in the region.

The Norwegian Public Road Administration has requested planning and engineering services for further development of the Bjørnafjord crossing, consisting of Front End Engineering Design (FEED) of a floating bridge solution.

The companies Aas-Jakobsen, COWI and Multiconsult have formed a Joint Venture to perform this task, involving the companies Aker Solutions, NGI, Entail, Dissing+Weitling and Moss Maritime as subconsultants. The project group is composed of companies with a long history in the Bjørnafjorden project, and with key personnel with unique experience from the marine and bridge field, many with a combination of both.

The overall target for FEED is to establish the required level of design in order to enter into a detail design phase for construction of the bridge.

## **3 Description, evaluations, and risk assessment of the base case solution**

#### **3.1 General**

A base case solution for FEED was developed in the initial phase of the work. This was based on the two K12 concepts from phase 5, with adjustments made in order to both:

- Utilize the better details from both concepts.

- Implement or evaluate review comments from NPRA and their cooperative entities to the phase 5 work.

- Utilize and implement revised design basis developed by NPRA including revised Metocean and supplementary soil investigations prepared prior to the FEED phase.

The adjustments to phase 5 concept have been discussed and agreed with NPRA and their cooperative entities.

It has been a prerequisite for the FEED work that the tower for the high bridge should be placed on Svarvahelleholmen and the road adaptable to the chosen road alignment from the ongoing zoning plan work given in the E39 Stord-Os zoning plan project. Based on this, a new optimized road line alignment has been developed.

The ship passage is located on the north side of Svarvahelleholmen, positioned so that no blasting for the ship channel will be required. The distance between centerline tower and centerline first pillar is chosen to be 430m. This provides satisfactory space for sailing routes with width 250 meters and free sailing height 45 meters. Documentation is given in memo (UO-§21)_10205546-01-NOT-318.

The road alignment is adjusted to comply with the one developed for the ongoing zoning plan. This alignment is optimized in the south and north to meet necessary requirements for the subsea tunnel and landscape adaptation respectively. In order to better the aesthetic appearance as well as traffic security, the road inclination from the floating bridge part towards the cable stayed part are reduced somewhat in FEED.

The width of the bridge deck is sought to be minimized in FEED, since this is found to be cost effective. This implies shoulders of 1.5m which is a deviation from the NPRA handbooks.

A more comprehensive ship collision risk analysis is performed after phase 5 of the project and is a part of the design basis. This also includes ship collision events with return period 100 years. This is governing for larger parts of the pontoons and columns, which has led to an increase in tonnage of these elements.

The revised design basis has a more comprehensive description of wind climate, waves and current than in phase 5. This has called for more detailed load analyses in FEED compared to earlier that affect the dimensions. For the southern part close to the tower, this has led to increased material consumption.

The overall boundary conditions of the base case is kept as in phase 5. This implies global fixation in both abutment and to the viaduct columns, vertical and horizontal sliding bearings at tower position as well as 12 supplementary anchors to the seabed. The anchors are placed as 2 anchors in axis 12,13,25 and 27 and 4 anchors in axis 19, supposed to 3 locations of 4 anchors in phase 5. These changes have been done to improve the risk of losing several anchors due to ship collisions. The subsea suction anchor positions are kept the same as in phase 5.

<!-- side pdf=19 -->

A revised requirement for fatigue within the design basis has called for more detailed fatigue analyses of the bridge girder. This has resulted in thicker deck plates than in phase 5 and adjusted detailing.

A comprehensive investigation of assembly methods and marine operations has been performed in FEED. The solution outlined by AMC in phase 5 involving a “sausage factory” in Søreidsvika where the whole floating bridge were supposed to be assembled in a floating condition , has been adjusted to a procedure of assembly of so called “super-elements” of about 500m length in non-floating conditions in Hanøytangen assembly yard, a towing operation of super-elements to Søreidsvika and connection of the superelements at that site before towing to the bridge site. The connection of the superelements to each other as well as the connection to adjacent structure at the bridge site, has been thoroughly analysed and designed to be a step vice procedure, see ch.13.

- SBJ-32-P1-SVV-90-BA-001 “Design Basis E39 Bjørnafjorden”, Rev C, 22.03.2023

- Road class = H3 (ÅDT>12 000) with adjusted shoulders.

- Road width = 2 x 9 meters plus 2.75m pedestrian/bike lane.

- Speed limit: 110 km/h.

- Sailing route (minimum): Height 45 meters, width 250 meters and depth 16.5 meters.

- The bridge is designed for a lifetime of 100 years.

- The bridge is being designed without a wind screen.

There are various challenges associated with the construction of a pontoon bridge over the Bjørnafjord. The concept is complex and requires the development of today's standard design methodology from both offshore and bridges. Through the work in the FEED phase, the concept and methodology have been brought forward, and significant uncertainties from earlier phases of the project have been removed. The construction itself has a degree of complexity that places special demands on expertise and experience for both design and construction. The construction is moreover planned on the basis of well-known operations, however used in a new context.

The FEED phase has further developed the concept to a significantly improved maturity. The environmental loads are more safely defined and the structure's behavior for different load situations well mapped for the different boundary conditions. The effect of different pontoon solutions has been studied in previous phases, and the concept's robustness for variation of spans between them and material choices in the pontoons has been demonstrated. On this basis, the floating bridge concept has been significantly further developed and brought up to a very good level of maturity.

In particular, a robust anchoring system based on anchoring five pontoons has been developed. The ground conditions on the seabed are mapped through several campaigns and the dimensions of anchors and anchors are found to be within ordinary offshore sizes for suction anchors. One anchor position is challenging with respect to available sediment thickness for suction anchors. This position is hence in the upper scale of “ordinary offshore sizes for suction anchors”. In AMCs experience somewhat larger diameter suction anchors/ suction caissons has however been installed previously.

The anchors are based on detailed assessments of seabed conditions and geotechnics as well as landslides. To counter the risk that landslides during earthquakes could knock out some anchor lines, the anchoring system has good redundancy for each anchored pontoon.

The bridge is made without expansion joints, and with only 4 bearings at the tower in axis 2.

The bridge is permanently tensioned to abutments at both ends.

Overall, the concept has been significantly developed and optimized in relation to the level of previous phases of the project. The present material provides a good basis for a detailed design phase, and also provides good accuracy of calculated quantities as a basis for calculating total cost.

##### **_3.2.2 Road alignment_**

The road alignment is designed for a fixed link consisting of a floating bridge combined with a cable stayed bridge across Bjørnafjorden from Reksteren in Tysnes Community to Gulholmane in Os Community.

The road outside the bridge is placed on rock ground or fillings of blasted rock. From south, the road has a 3 km long road on land between a long tunnel and the bridge crossing Bjørnafjorden. From

<!-- side pdf=22 -->

Gulholmane there is a filling and the road goes directly into a tunnel under Røtinga and Søre Øyane. The tunnels on each side give plenty of blasted rock on each side of the bridge available for fillings.

The road design class is found to be H3 according to Handbook N100 (2022), but with reduced shoulders in accordance with Design Basis .

The stop sight as it is defined in N100 (2022) determines the minimum vertical radius to R=11000m. In the south a large vertical radius on the cable stayed bridge of R=35000 m is used. This solves the sight problems that could be the result from tide and traffic load on a vertical curve at the start of the floating bridge.

The slope from the level of the cable stayed bridge down to the constant level over Bjørnafjorden at elevation +18 is 3 %. 5 % is maximum slope for this road class, but it is not advisable to use this maximum slope if you could avoid it. In this case we have large curves and 3D-studies show that 3 % is more in harmony with the other dimensions.

The concave vertical curve when the slope of 3 % meet the horizontal level has a radius R=35000. This is much larger than in earlier stages (R=5000 m or R=18000). This as desired from the architect in order to improve aesthetics. It is also believed that it has a positive effect on both traffic safety and comfort.

In the north, the road will go down into a tunnel under the sea, and this situation means that the vertical curve will start on the bridge and that the height at the abutment is about 5-6 m lower than the constant level of +18. This happens in the same area as the tide and the traffic load on the floating bridge will reduce the stop sight length. The minimum vertical curve should not be used where tide on a daily basis reduces the stop sight length. The vertical curve in the north is therefore increased to R=20000 m, larger than in earlier stages.

##### **_3.2.9.2 Stays_**

The pontoons are designed in the same way as for ships, i.e., with braced plate fields, frames/spuns and internal watertight bulkheads.

The pontoon has two main functions; to provide buoyancy and stability to the bridge, and to act as a collision buffer to achieve a predictable response in the bridge in the event of a ship impact. The pontoon will within reasonable limits always be weaker than a striking ship, and the design is thus optimized to reduce the direct and indirect consequences of an impact event rather than to avoid damage. The main changes compared to the previous design phases of the crossing are:

<!-- side pdf=33 -->

1. The volumes of the internal water-tight compartments are reduced to limit the loss of buoyancy and flooded volume.

2. Permanent water ballast is placed in each pontoon, so that in the event of flooding the ballast can be removed and the buoyancy can be restored. With this. the bridge girder will be less affected by an impact and the downtime is reduced accordingly. Furthermore, it gives a larger contingency for weight increases during the lifetime of the bridge.

3. The structure is optimized to have sufficient capacity for conventional loads while also reducing the contact force between ship and pontoon. Thus, forces transferred further into the bridge structure are minimized.

In the event of a ship impact, the pontoon will have to be repaired.

All steel panels outside and internal tanks must be waterproof and are designed for maximum water pressure in combination with ULS loads. The steel plates have thicknesses varying from 12mm to 16mm and are braced with bulb steel stiffeners spaced typically 800mm. The pontoons are divided into a large number of compartments, typically sized to be 5 x 3.8m.

In the splash zone, the external steel skin is replaced with a corrosion resistant material type 25Cr duplex ("super duplex"). This is done to limit the need for maintenance. The height of this plate field is set to be from 1m below the still water line up to the top deck, 4.5m in total.

Carbon steel in air is corrosion protected and maintained with paint systems such as the rest of the bridge. Unmaintainable carbon steel under water is corrosion protected with zinc paint systems and sacrificial anodes. The number of sacrificial anodes must be increased as the paint system decomposes.

Four internal compartments are used for permanent water ballast, but 24 compartments are used temporarily during the construction phase (in which the pontoons, columns and the bridge girder are assembled into smaller bridge units at Hanøytangen). There will be permanent access to each compartment within the pontoon, but this is closed during normal operation. Ballasting is performed on six groups of four tanks each during the temporary phases via one opening for each group through the top deck of the pontoon and internal access within the compartments in each group. Once the bridge is fully assembled, the internal openings between the grouped compartments are bolted tight. The piping system used for the temporary phases is kept during the lifetime of the bridge. However, as the internal access is closed, the system only interfaces with a single compartment pr. group. This then allows for re-ballasting operations if needed during the life cycle of the bridge. Steel for internal access and ballast piping is accounted for as a gross increase in the pontoon weights.

Pontoons that are permanently moored are reinforced with additional internal structural elements that support an external fairlead to the bottom of the pontoon. The fairleads and reinforcements are dimensioned according to the breaking strength of the mooring line, ensuring a higher capacity than the line itself.

#### **3.6 Evaluation of the base case solution**

The base case solution is a result of further development of the two parallel concept recommendations made in phase 5 of the project for the K12 concept. In this chapter, the base case solution is evaluated based on this development, the reasons behind the development and the consequences for the design.

##### **_3.6.1 Stay cable bridge_**

The main span of stay cable bridge has increased from 380m in phase 5 to 430m in the FEED study. The reason for this is that the ship channel is positioned at a further distance from

Svarvahelleholmen so that no blasting will be required.  The larger span length will still be possible to build as a free cantilever without temporary support during construction. The bridge girder will be supported sideways at tower as in phase 5. The back span and viaduct are supported on piers monolithically connected to the bridge girder. The pier closest to the tower will experience large transverse forces, especially from wind loading. In order to reduce the response, this pier has been made hollow and a reduced stiffness for cracked concrete is used in the global analysis when relevant.

##### **_3.6.2 Width of bridge girder_**

In phase 5 of the project, the total width of the road profile including pedestrian lane was 23.5 m, while the chosen width of the bridge girder was 27m i.e., somewhat more wide. This girder width was largely based on the required width of the K11 alternative (this alternative had no mooring lines). In earlier phases of the project investigations showed that it was feasible to decrease the width when mooring lines were present, though in phase 5 such an optimization was not performed for the recommended alternative K12 with mooring lines.

In the FEED phase, the width of road profile has been reduced to 22.25 m. It was decided to reduce the width of girder as much as possible since the mentioned earlier investigation indicated beneficial effects for strong axis dynamic behavior. The width of the top plate is set to 22.75 m in FEED phase. In order to have an aerodynamic shape, the width of bottom plate is as a consequence reduced from 16m to 12m and a structural wind nose is introduced. It can be argued that a reduced width of bottom plate with inclined plates with a small angle are not beneficial for the weak axis response in the girder for the floating bridge. However, this is being treated as a tradeoff with respect to increased wind loading giving increased strong axis moment. In future work it could be investigated if a less aerodynamic shape giving a stronger section for weak axis moment, could be beneficial for the girder in the floating bridge.  All in all, the decreased width of girder will reduce the steel weight of girder. However, this reduction is counteracted by the increased plate thickness in the top plate due to an updated, more severe fatigue traffic model, giving in total a marginal saving in steel girder weight.

The average strong axis stiffness for the low part of floating bridge (weighted average between support area and mid span), has decreased by approximately 8% from I=92 m<sup>4</sup> in phase 5 to I =85 m<sup>4</sup> in the FEED phase. The cross-section area of the girder is nearly unchanged, while the weak axis stiffness has increased by 17% and 33% for support section and mid span section respectively.

##### **_3.6.3 Pontoons_**

The number of pontoons has been reduced from 38 in phase 5 to 37 in the FEED phase. This is due to an increased length of main span from 380 m to 430 m and a general increase in pontoon center distance from 125m to 126.8m, and some minor adjustment in the position of the tower in south and

<!-- side pdf=45 -->

the abutment in north. The freeboard of 3.5 m has been kept from phase 5, this has shown to be adequate. However, the freeboard of the pontoons in axis 3 and axis 39 will be influenced by the tidal water and ought therefore to have an increased freeboard of approximately 20-40 cm in future updates of the concept. The draft has been increased by 0.3 -0.5 m due to a refined division of watertight compartments and due to reserve ballast. The length of pontoons has been increased from 53m to 56m in the high bridge area in order to increase the roll stability for traffic loads which was not satisfactory in phase 5. The adapted roll criterion of 1.0 degree due to traffic loads, will ensure a good overall roll stability of the bridge. When this is satisfied, it can be shown that the collapse load due to roll instability will be larger than the load required for loosing the freeboard of 3.5 m.

For the pontoons anchored by mooring lines, the draft has been reduced from 7.5m to 6.5m. In phase 5, the fairlead was considered not to be directly hit by ships with draft less than 6m. This has now been reconsidered in the ship impact design, and a fairlead connection placed higher has thereby been accepted. Also, the fairlead connection shall be placed below the super duplex steel area, which is still possible with a draft of 6.5 m.

##### **_3.6.4 Positioning of mooring lines_**

The starting point of positioning the mooring line anchors is the phase 5 (2019) geohazard and geotechnical considerations. In phase 5 all anchors were suction anchors located on a relatively flat seabed with suitable sediment type and thickness for installation and stability. The geohazard screening analysis in 2019 was essentially the same as performed in stage 3 (2017). These were based on available soil investigations and bathymetry mapping from 2016.

In stage 5 the anchors were placed in optimal positions with respect to geohazard aspects from the stage 3 study. These positions were therefore the starting point in the FEED study performed in 2023 (stage 7). To improve symmetry of the mooring layout and response of the bridge, minor shifts of anchor positions from the stage 5 positions have been introduced for some anchors. These shifts are up to 100 m compared to the 2019 locations, while adjusting the actual positions, the results from the 2019 geohazard study are given attention.

Updated geohazard calculations in the FEED stage have been based on supplementary soil investigations from 2019 and detailed bathymetry mapping in the vicinity of the stage 5 anchor positions from 2022. Due to the unfavorable results from the latest soil investigation campaign, the updated geohazard calculations are more critical than the previous stage 5 study. Some of the FEED anchor positions are therefore still associated with a medium risk level. Several mitigations and suggestions to way forward are provided in Appendix U.

Summarized, based on the above, the current anchor locations were optimized based on the geohazard study from 2017. Anchor placement is a trade-off between optimal anchor placement with respect to the bridge and the risk of loss of anchors and its consequence for the global bridge response. When applying this philosophy in the FEED study we see a significant improvement in the global bridge response. We also see a robust bridge concept that can cope with the project safety requirements regarding loss of several anchors.

In the FEED phase, 5 pontoons are anchored, compared to only 3 in phase 5, although the number of mooring lines is still the same, 12 in total. The reason for distributing the mooring lines to more pontoons is to reduce the risk of damaging more than one mooring line during a possible ship impact. The disadvantage with this solution, implying only two mooring lines all together at one pontoon, is that it gives more restriction on the foundation site, since the position must be such that

the pretension forces of mooring lines are in equilibrium (with each other). This is easier to obtain if 4 mooring lines are attached to the same pontoon. In the FEED phase, 2 lines are connected to pontoon in axes 12,13 25 and 27, while 4 lines are connected to axis 19. The reason for having 4 lines to the pontoon in axis 19, is because it is a challenge to find suitable locations for the suction anchors and still keep equilibrium for the pretension forces as mentioned above, if the mooring lines are distributed to two pontoons instead of one.

In the initial stage of FEED, the position of mooring lines was discussed thoroughly. The purpose of the clusters in north and in south are to contribute to the modal stiffness for mode 1, and in this way reduce the eigen period significantly. The mode shapes further indicate that the position of anchor lines in south and in north could be more optimal if they were moved further out towards the cable stayed bridge and the abutment in north respectively. However, in the north, this would likely mean that gravity anchors would have to be used instead of suction anchors. In the south, it might be possible to move the anchor further south and still find some locations suited for suction anchors. However, this will require a revised evaluation of undersea landslides due to earthquakes for the new locations as well as a more detailed evaluation of the suitability of the locations from soil thickness etc. In the FEED phase it has therefore been decided to choose the relatively safe location as described.

For mode 1, the length from axis 2 to axis 19 where zero movement occurs is calculated to be 2459m. The optimal position of mooring is approximately at 0.4 L from axis 19, i.e., equal to 984 m. The corresponding chosen distance to anchor in the south is approximately 824 m. Thus, it is not believed to gain very much by moving this cluster, considering the eigen period for mode 1. However, for the effect from wind and especially mean wind, moving the anchor further south e.g., to axis 8, would be beneficial since the wind load increases on the girder in the high bridge and on the high columns giving large strong axis moments in the girder at the tower position.

The initial tangential stiffness of mooring lines is determined to be approximately 300 KN/m for a pair of mooring lines, giving 600 kN/m for a cluster of 4 mooring lines. The eigen periods of the two first modes are dominated by this mooring stiffness. This can be shown by simplified hand calculations. Mode 3 and 4 are influenced both by girder stiffness and mooring stiffness, while mode 5 and upwards are dominated by girder stiffness. The chosen stiffness of 600 kN/m limits the transverse movement of 100 years mean response to approximately 4m and 100 years dynamic response to approximately 4m. Both mean and dynamic response are dominated by wind. A higher stiffness of anchor line will in the end suppress mode 1 giving mode 2. Thus, it seems to be beneficial to increase this stiffness to a certain extent.  However, it has been an aim to limit the number of mooring lines, and with the chosen concept a higher pretension giving higher stiffness will reduce the damping. Lower damping will increase the dynamic response from wind.

The mid-cluster will reduce the eigen period of mode 2 considerably due to contribution from stiffness. In addition, the cluster will generate damping for this mode. Thus, the mid-cluster will contribute to reducing the response from dynamic wind loading. It will also have a beneficial effect on parametric excitation. Due to the explanations above it has therefore been chosen to keep the mid cluster as initially proposed.

The structural behavior of the floating bridge is quite flexible regarding relocation of the anchor positions in the transverse direction of the bridge axis. It is also quite flexible for change in location in longitudinal direction of the bridge within 200 m. In this case some additional static forces may be induced into the column and bridge girder which will be feasible to design for.

##### **_3.6.5 Abutment North_**

The last span towards abutment in north, has been chosen to be the same span length as for the other spans, 126.8m. A longer span would give a lower response from tidal water. On the other side a longer span will give larger response from permanent load and traffic loads. It was decided that equal span was most appropriate also from a construction point of view. Results show that weak axis moment with tension in top of girder is governing for the design. Therefore, one option may be to increase the buoyancy in the pontoon in axis 39 to induce weak axis moment with compression in top of girder. This will require a pontoon with larger draft and the girder must be produced with correct camber to achieve the final alignment. Further investigation of this item is recommended for future work.

##### **_3.6.6 Assembly and marine operations_**

Assembly and marine operations of the floating bridge part were outlined as high-risk elements in phase 5 of the project. Large efforts have therefore made in the FEED phase to mitigate these risk elements through adjusted methods, involving a widespread of competence from adequate markets and documentation of the process and the corresponding structural behavior of the affected structural parts.

In the initial stage of FEED, the construction sequence in question was discussed between the parties involved, and the assumed favorable parts investigated by the two design groups in phase 5 were evaluated up against each other. SVV pointed out Hanøytangen to be a base case solution for assembly of parts of the floating bridge. This resulted in the proposed assembly method of superelements of app. 500m length in this yard that is documented in FEED and verified through the owner of the yard as well as adequate vendors of equipment and operation. This also resulted in the use of Søreidsvika as a temporary site to connect the super-elements to each other before towing to the bridge site.

One critical stage of the marine operations is found to be at a holding position close to the bridge site. Due to the very long period dynamic behavior of the bridge in this situation, there is a demand for advanced and correlated support from a number of tugboats. Efforts should be made to further mitigate this stage through supplementary anchoring and/or simulations.

A lot of effort is made on describing and documenting the various stages in the connecting procedure between the super-elements while floating. This involves both how to match and align the two counterparts, temporary fixations and the corresponding welding procedure where each part is welded to the neighboring part. These procedures are repeated both in Søreidsvika and in Bjørnafjorden, however with different load levels. The goal has been to document a feasible method to be used. The method involves a temporary hinge in the upper part of the bridge deck that can withstand storm conditions until a weather window is available for the welding procedure to be carried out. There are a number of work operations required within each weather window to be used, and the time consumption for these operations as well as the number of preparations that can be done prior to the weather window, are of vital importance. There will be alternative methods to the proposed procedure that may be investigated further as well as further mitigations and backup solutions to be documented before tender.

##### **_3.7.1 Requirement for temporary and permanent ballast, contingency and hatches in top plate of pontoons_**

According to the Design basis, permanent ballast shall be included to account for deviations in weight (contingency). In current design 150 tons ballast has been allocated to account for such contingency. This corresponds to approximately 4% of the final weight of the structure including weight from asphalt and railings for the lower part of bridge (150/4000= 3.75%). For future design phase, it should be evaluated if the contingency also shall include an uncertainty in the calculation of the steel weight done in an early stage.  This can be relevant if the contractors want to freeze the dimensions of the pontoons for production purposes before the final calculations of steel amounts are finalized. In this case the dimension and required buoyancy of the pontoons must be based on a preliminary weight estimate and some uncertainty must thus be included. The 4% contingency is not meant to cover uncertainty due to early design, but rather minor changes due to construction tolerances in steel amount, deviations in asphalt layer, extra equipment on bridge and minor changes in design. A deviation of 10 mm in asphalt layer constitutes approximately 60 tons per pontoon.

In the FEED study it has been decided to include additional permanent ballast with the purpose of redistributing the ballast in case of an accidental situation where pontoon compartment is flooded due to ship impact. By re-distributing the ballast, the pontoon can nearly regain its position such that the bridge can be kept open for traffic until it is convenient to repair the damaged pontoon, for instance during a summer period. An amount of 400 tons permanent ballast is allocated for this purpose. Altogether approximately 550 tons of permanent water ballast is used in the FEED study distributed to 4 permanent ballast compartment each with a hatch in top. It is planned to use fresh water for permanent ballast.

During construction phases it is required to use temporary ballast allocated to 24 compartments. It is assumed to use fresh water for temporary ballasting. In order to reduce the number of hatches in the top plate some of the ballasting compartments are connected together giving in total 10 hatches. These connections will be closed before the float out from Hanøytangen. The remaining ballasting operation after leaving Hanøytangen must be done with the 10 compartments with hatches.

During installation at Hanøytangen, the first pontoon with column must be ballasted down on the underwater supports. This will require 3300 tons to 4000 tons depending on the pontoon type. After leaving Hanøytangen the amount of temporary ballast will correspond to the weight of asphalt plus equipment giving approximately 600 tons for each pontoon. In addition, temporary ballast may be used during connection of bridge elements at Søreidsvika and in Bjørnafjorden during final installation.

As mentioned, 10 hatches will be used in the top plate of pontoons with the purpose of moving temporary ballast water efficiently in and out of the pontoons. 4 of these hatches will be permanent. The rest can be removed or permanently locked after the bridge is finalized in Bjørnafjorden.

##### **_3.7.2 Wind load on vehicles in ULS and FLS limit states_**

In the global analysis wind load on vehicles has not been included. The reason for this is that weak axis moment is governed by short influence lines with high traffic load intensity. The governing influence lines are often shorter than 200 m. It would therefore be unreasonable to put wind load on vehicles over the whole 5 km long bridge. The option of putting wind load on vehicle on the corresponding influence length will give an unpractical large number of runs for the time domain analysis. For weak axis moment the error of omitting the wind load on vehicle is regarded to be small. For roll movement of the bridge the influence lines are long, and, in this case, it would be reasonable to include wind load on vehicle. AMC is currently performing additional work to investigate certain load cases regarding robustness among which one of them is the investigation of wind load on vehicle placed along the bridge.

For the global analysis used to determine fatigue response from vehicles, no wind load on vehicles is used. The reason for this is that the number of heavy vehicles on Bjørnafjorden bridge is relatively small. Using an ÅDT of 19 000 and heavy traffic of 10% will give one vehicle on the bridge every 45 seconds assuming traffic in one direction. This will give a distance between heavy vehicles of 900 m when traveling at a speed of 80 km/h. Even for regular cars the distance will be approximately 90 m. Thus, it is more correct to ignore the wind load on vehicle for fatigue calculations.

##### **_3.7.3 Design for EQU (static equilibrium) in ultimate limit state._**

In the global analysis weight from traffic load is not included. Traffic loads are run in the program RM-bridge where modified waterplane stiffness has been used considering only the permanent load of the structure. RM- bridge with such a linearization has been used since the program automatically can generate traffic load cases based on the influence length and corresponding traffic load intensity. Then the superposition principle can be used giving a combination file including all possible distribution of traffic loads.  In order to do this in Orcaflex a large number of load cases must be established and if the weight of traffic for each distribution should be accounted for, the superposition principle will no longer hold resulting in a large number of analyses to be run.

For the weak axis moment short influence lines will be governing and thus the effect of weight from vehicles is considered to be small. However, for certain responses the influence lines are long, such as for the roll of the bridge.  In this case the effect of the weight of vehicles is more important.

Additional work is at presently carried out to investigate the effect of the weight from vehicles on certain responses. This can be considered to be a check of the static equilibrium in ultimate limit state EQU, according to NS-EN 1990. In EQU combination the traffic weight will be factorized by 1.35 in the analysis. The load intensity from traffic is approximately 4 ton/m when the whole bridge length is loaded and 5.4 with the load factor of 1.35.  The permanent weight of the girder is approximately 19 tons/m. The critical buckling capacity for the roll mode is a factor of approximately 2.4 relative to the deck weight of 19 tons/m. This value will be verified in the additional work presently carried out. The second order effects from large deformation will amplify the response and can roughly be calculated as following:

1/(1-1/2.4) = 1.71.

This amplification is already included in the RM-analysis using modified water plane stiffness.  In Orcaflex this effect is also automatically included. For a bridge fully loaded in load situation EQU the amplification will be approximately:

1/(1-(19+5.4)/(19 x 2.4))= 2.15.

<!-- side pdf=50 -->

Thus, in this case the amplification increases from 1.7 to 2.15 due to the extra weight from traffic. However, this situation will not be governing for roll since the traffic is nearly centered and will give small 1. order roll.  In the case of eccentric traffic along the whole bridge the total weight is approximately 3 ton/m and 4 ton/m with load factor in EQU. The corresponding amplification will then be approximately 2.0 which is an increase of 18 % from the run performed in global analysis. Note that the criterion for roll in SLS is 1 degree for characteristic traffic load multiplied by 0.7 when the traffic is run within the traffic lanes and not on the shoulder. For a load factor of 1.35 the roll will then become 1.92 and approximately 2.2 degrees when run on shoulder. Thus, it is expected that the roll increases from approximately 2.2 degrees in the present analysis in ULS to 2.6 degrees when considering the vehicle loads in an EQU load situation which is deemed aceptable. As mentioned, AMC is presently performing analysis including the vehicle weight to verify this behavior.

##### **_3.7.4 Marine fouling_**

The submerged weight of marine fouling is according to design basis 48 kg/m2. The submerged surface area of the walls is for the lower bridge pontoons approximately 652 m2 and the bottom plate 742 m2. The total weight of the marine fouling on bottom plate and side walls is therefore 67 tons. This will deflect the pontoon by 9 cm. This is a variable load, and the deflection is considered to be small compared to all other loads and is therefore not included in the global analysis.

##### **_3.7.5 Shear lag effects in the global beam analysis_**

According to clause 2.2(1)P in NS-EN 1993-1-5, the effects of shear lag on the stiffness of members shall be taken into account in the global analysis. However, according to clause 2.2(3), the effectives width of flanges should be taken as the lesser of the full width and L/8 per side of the web, where L is the span length equal to 126.8 m. Then 2 ∗ 126.8 𝑚 /8=31.7 𝑚 will be larger than the full width of the largest plate, the top plate of 22.8m, and therefore the full width can be applied in the global beam analysis. This is applicable to static actions typically coming from permanent and traffic loads. For dynamic analysis, shearlag effect may influence the eigen period and thus the response. Shear lag effects will influence the shortest eigen periods mostly. The shortest eigen period which induces significant dynamic response into the bridge structure is the so-called piston mode where one pontoon is moving up and the next down. The typical eigen period is approximately 3.5 seconds for this mode. A finite element analysis has been established to determine the shear lag effects of this mode giving a reduction in stiffness of approximately 10 % which will then change the eigen period by approximately 5% which is not considered to be significant. For modes with higher periods there will be nearly no shear lag effects. Thus, it is concluded that it is not necessary to include shear lag effects in the global analysis neither for static nor dynamic responses.   However, shear lag effects will be included when calculating stresses in the girder from the forces from the global analyses.

##### **_3.7.6 Fatigue load model for traffic_**

Fatigue load model 5 (FLM5) as applied in the Langenuen and Julsundet bridge projects is used in design. Langenuen bridge is located close to Bjørnafjorden, and long-distance traffic crossing Langenuen bridge will also have to cross the Bjørnafjorden bridge.

The Norwegian Public Roads Administration (NPRA) has developed a probabilistic approach of establishing a site-specific fatigue load model 5 (FLM5) based on three months of Weigh-in-Motion (WIM) data at Eikås in 2019. The result is the traffic load model for fatigue that is described in the Design Basis “SBJ-32-P1-SVV-90-BA-001-C Design Basis E39 Bjørnafjorden”. In order to facilitate a more rapid and practically applicable fatigue analysis, a deterministic FLM5 was established during the Langenuen project. The development of the new FLM5 is presented in the technical note made

by Arup “277806-19-FNO-01, Rev 2, Development of a Fatigue Load Model 5 (FLM5) for the Langenuen and Julsundet suspension bridges. The technical note also includes a similar site-specific fatigue load model 5 for the new Julsundet suspension bridge near Molde, based on WIM-data at Sæterbøen and Horgheim (Romsdalen) in 2022.

There is a significant difference in the truck distribution between the load models of the two locations. The Julsundet load model predominantly shows trucks with 5 or more axles, whereas the Langenuen load model consists of mostly trucks with 4 or fewer axles. A similar change in truck types is shown in other load models (for example FLM4 according to NS-EN-1991-2) where a distinction is made between “local traffic” resulting in more small trucks, and “long-distance traffic” resulting in more large trucks. This would indicate that potentially the Eikås WIM data sees more “local traffic” than the WIM location in Romsdalen, and it could be questioned if this “local traffic” is indeed representative for the Langenuen (and Bjørnafjorden) crossing. As a consequence of these differences, comparisons indicate that the order of the different load models with respect to increasing fatigue damage (1 is lowest and 3 is largest) are:

1. Probabilistic FLM5 based on WIM data at Eikås, described in the Design Basis from SVV.

2. Deterministic FLM5 based on WIM data at Eikås, described in 277806-19-FNO-01.

3. Deterministic FLM5 based on WIM data at Romsdalen, described in 277806-19-FNO-01.

For Langenuen bridge, it was chosen to apply the truck distribution determined for Julsundet bridge (number 3 above), but with the traffic numbers according to Langenuen (AADT of 19 000, 10% heavy traffic amount), which are the same as for Bjørnafjorden.

It is reasonable to design according to the same fatigue load model for Bjørnafjorden and Langenuen, and this will be conservative compared to using the fatigue load model described in the Design Basis from NPRA.

In order to account for a future trend of increasing axle weights, an axle weight trend factor of 1.2 during the design life is included as it was for Langenuen bridge. This trend factor is interpreted as a weight amplification factor which increases linearly to a value of 1.2 after 100 years. This can be implemented by calculating factors for each of the 100 years (1.002, 1.004, …, 1.198, 1.2) and obtain the fatigue damage for each year. Alternatively, in order to limit the number of fatigue damage calculations, the 100 years can be split up into larger time periods (e.g. 10 or 50 years) and fatigue damage is calculated for each time period using the largest amplification factor within the period. This approach will be conservative.

For more details refer to document “Fatigue analysis description, 10205546-13-NOT-324”.

The local wind sea, governed by the fetch length and wind velocity, may be sensitive to small change in the wind direction. #### **3.8 Recommendations for further work, further optimizations, and possible variations**

The work performed in the FEED phase has revealed that adjustments to the design layout or supplementary work may be appropriate in order to either reduce investments cost, maintenance costs or risk. This work can be done as a part of finalizing the FEED phase or as preparation work prior to tender issues. This work is further described below in a proposed prioritized order:

- Supplementary investigations on mitigation actions in design following a possible negative conclusion on robustness checks due to submarine slides that are being performed after issue of rev. 0 of this report.

- One critical stage of the marine operations is found to be the towing phase from Søreidsvika. This operation is performed in a weather window, but due to the flexible girder with very long periods large movements can be initiated from dynamic wind loading even for winds as low as 8 m/s. This stage should be further analysed and if possible perform simulations of the towing operation with operation crew involved.

- Another critical stage of the marine operations is found to be at a holding position close to the bridge site when the bridge must withstand a 10 year summer storm. Due to the very long period dynamic behavior of the bridge in this situation, there is a demand for advanced and correlated support from a number of tugboats. Efforts should be made to further mitigate potential risk during this stage, for instance through simulations.

- Optimization of floating bridge support area in north, including load analysis, further detailing of transition piece and evaluation of increased freeboard for pontoon axis 39. This will most probably lead to lower construction costs due to less congestion of steel and posttension in the abutment area.

- Fatigue calculations of mooring lines should be re-investigated in a next phase.

- Moving the light poles to the east side of the bridge may reduce the total width of the bridge and thereby be cost effective. This activity is related to final choice of road class/speed limit that may affect the project requirements. When this is settled, the minimum bridge deck width can be established and the tower leg configuration and bridge deck details at the same spot can be optimized.

- Evaluation of moving of anchors from axis 13 to axis 8. This will reduce the governing reactions in the tower area and thereby has the potential to reduce costs. Possible uncertainties with regards to the new anchor locations need to be adressed.

- Supplementary geological evaluations are recommended as risk mitigation. A few recommendations for further work with regards to ship collision are given below:

- Local impact simulations have been performed for impacts perpendicular to the shell of the pontoon at the impact location. This is the impact direction with the highest potential for large indentation to the hull, but other impact directions may yield different damage scenarios that may or may not be as critical. From a global point of view, impacts that are not perpendicular may cause a yaw motion of the ship that cause it to deflect during the impact (a glancing impact). This challenges the validity of the split between internal and external dynamics, and the ship and bridge motion thus must be considered directly in the local simulations.

   - It is recommended to study this further for two reasons:

      - An impact that is more along than towards the pontoon may cause a long horizontal rupture as the bulb scrapes the pontoon hull, causing significant flooding.

      - The global impact simulations were performed with an assumption of the outer-most point in which a ship will stick to the pontoon (outside of this point, a glancing impact will occur, and the ship is deflected away from the pontoon). This is a sensitive assumption, as the maximum distance from the column that the ship may stick is directly related to the torsional response of the column during impact. Coupled local-global simulations are recommended to assess this assumption and recommend a good starting point for detailed design.

- The effect of the mass and velocity relation had some effect on the bridge girder and column responses. For detailed design it is recommended to increase the simulation matrix sufficiently so that variations can be captured.

- High responses were observed in the back-span columns of the cable-stayed bridge due to both 100- and 10 000-year impact events. Including load factors, the 100-year impact events were dimensioning for parts of the back-span piers. This phenomenon should be further studied to ensure that it is captured in an adequate manner for both global and local response.

- The bridge girder towards the Northern abutment is dimensioned by the strong-axis bending moment due to deckhouse impact to the two northernmost spans between pontoons. Here, the bridge is stiffness-dominated, and increasing the stiffness of the bridge to resist the impact increases the impact load that the bridge must withstand. Hence, it is vital to ensure that the bridge girder is not overly stiff in this area. It is recommended to study this further with a nonlinear collapse analysis of the bridge girder subjected to sectional loads surpassing its capacity. Can some damage be allowed, or is the collapse mechanism so abrupt that even 10 000-year impact events should be managed within the elastic capacity of the girder? If the study results in a reduced stiffness of the cross-section, the design loads will be reduced, and a positive spiral of cost-reduction can be achieved for both the bridge girder and the Northern abutment.

- As the bridge girder and Northern abutment is dimensioned by deckhouse impacts towards the Northern end of the bridge, more work should be done to assess the deckhouse load through studying the deckhouse structures of the relevant vessels in the area. If required, the deckhouse finite element model should be revised, after which the resulting bridge response can be reevaluated.

#### **4.1 Introduction**

A thorough architectural evaluation has been performed on the preferred bridge concept.

This assessment primarily involves assessing the visual appeal of various elements within both the floating bridge and cable-stayed bridge sections, as well as the surrounding landscaping.

Additionally, this evaluation encompasses the overall alignment of the bridge concept.

The work is also based on the architectural evaluations performed in phase 5 of the project by the same architectural team.

Architectural visualizations are gathered in Appendix C and note 10205546-07-NOT-195.

#### **4.2 Alignment**

Ensuring a seamless integration with the surrounding landscape is a crucial consideration for both road and bridge alignment.

Given the breathtaking beauty of the Bjørnafjorden area, it becomes particularly essential to extend the bridge alignment as a natural continuation of the approaching road network, seamlessly merging with the landscape’s rhythm.

When observed from the shoreline, the bridge should artfully mirror the land’s contours, creating a harmonious connection with the existing environment.

The selected C-curve alignment offers a straightforward representation that gracefully emulates the structural equilibrium found in the expansive waterscape.

This curvature harmoniously resonates with the concept of a buoyant element, reminiscent of a tensioned “Bow.”

<!-- side pdf=59 -->

As an effect, it presents a captivating tableau of the main span and tower when approached from the north.

As drivers traverse the bridge, they are instinctively guided toward these key focal points, creating an effortless navigational experience.

The integration of a horizontal C-curve with a seamlessly transitioning vertical profile plays a pivotal role in delivering a cohesive and visually pleasing alignment experience.

The transition point between the cable-stayed bridge and the lower floating bridge incorporates a 3% inclination, paired with the largest feasible vertical transition curve. This combination works to establish a comprehensive overview, ensuring both safety and aesthetic appeal throughout the driving journey.

Moreover, this modest incline contributes to enhancing the conditions for pedestrians and bicyclists who engage with the connection, further elevating the overall experience.

While approaching from the south, drivers undergo a dramatic revelation of the main span, tower, fjord, and floating bridge as they navigate through terrain cuts onto the bridge.

This transition provides a truly memorable and awe-inspiring perspective.

#### **4.3 Cable stayed bridge**

The southern landing features an inclined vertical profile and a single-sided navigational span of 430m, resulting in an asymmetrical bridge design.

The arrangement of cable stays fans out asymmetrically in a ratio of about 3:2 across the tower which effectively highlights the bridge’s asymmetrical configuration.

The backspan, at deck level, combines a tightly spaced cable arrangement connected to the concrete viaduct with a more open arrangement across the steel deck.

This configuration extends over the main span, emphasizing the suspended bridge girder as it spans over the coastline.

#### **4.4 Tower**

A prominently positioned A-shaped tower emerges as the most logical choice for the cable-stayed bridge, effectively addressing both structural and aesthetic considerations.

The inherently pointed design of the A-shaped tower effortlessly captures attention within the landscape, emitting a strong visual ”signal.”

The outwardly flared legs beneath the deck adeptly tackle the horizontal forces that the deck must contend with.

Situated on Svarvahelleholmen, adjacent to the navigational clearance area, the tower’s placement presents an opportunity for tapered tower legs to extend to the edges of a small lagoon which will be land filled to cover the large foundations.

The tower displays intricate detailing, notably in the substantial chamfering of its lower section and the upper portion above the deck.

These refinements create a gracefully slender profile by artfully interplaying shadows and light at various times of the day.

The basecase tower incorporates a sizable slot beneath the deck designed for maintenance gantry parking.

Subsequent investigations have demonstrated that inspection using a ”cherry picker” is feasible, prompting the development of an alternative tower design with a reduced gap underneath the deck.

#### **4.5 Bridge girder**

To maintain an aesthetically slender appearance, a tapered design has been chosen for the bridge girder. The profiles of the bridge girder segments on both the floating and cable-stayed sections of the bridge have been synchronized.

This, combined with the seamlessly integrated wind nose feature, ensures a continuous visual flow throughout the entire length of the bridge.

Between the concrete viaduct, the cable-stayed bridge segment, and the steel sections of the floating bridge, a 24-meter transition exists due to the expansion required for the cable anchorages.

Approaching both the north and south abutments, the wind nose gradually undergoes a transformation over a 5-meter span, evolving into a vertical edge that seamlessly merges with the abutment walls.

#### **4.6 Piers**

Precise care is dedicated to shaping the viaduct piers, with the intention of establishing a distinct visual coherence with the tower and floating piers.

The chamfering technique sustains an uninterrupted visual link with both the girder and tower designs, effectively utilizing the interplay of light and shadow in a consistent manner.

The pontoons have been designed as rounded rectangular shapes to effectively handle the substantial forces exerted by the sea.

The considerable 126.8-meter span between the floating piers creates an extraordinarily open structure, prominently visible from various distant viewpoints encircling Bjørnafjorden.

#### **4.7 Abutments**

Both the northern and southern abutments showcase substantial solidity, achieved through robust connections from the floating bridge and the utilization of gravity fill. The sides facing the bridge are chamfered, forging a strong visual connection with the bridge structure.

Furthermore, the retaining walls for the secondary platforms and walkways are constructed using native rock tiles, ensuring a harmonious integration of design language and materials with the adjacent landscape.

Positioned on Gulholmen, the northern abutment, responsible for securing the massive floating arch, incorporates a service entrance accessible via a semi-internal stairway leading to the bridge level.

On the southern abutment, a maintenance drive-in level is incorporated for vehicles tasked with servicing the bridge’s internal components.

This space facilitates the transfer of large elements onto the deck.

To facilitate access, a service road needs to be established connecting to the already present construction service road.

The preference for curved and soft contours ensures a harmonious integration with the surrounding landscape.

## **5 Global analysis results**

#### **5.1 Governing responses**

For the bridge girder the governing combinations for ULS has been found to be the intact combinations ULS2 (governing traffic load with 1-year environmental loads) and ULS3 (governing 100-year environmental loads without traffic loads) and the ULS combinations for 10-2 collision scenarios. ULS3 has been found governing for the stay cable bridge and in the high part of the floating bridge, as well as towards the north end of the bridge. The response for ULS3 is dominated by the wind and wave loading with some contribution from temperature (strong axis bending). In addition, permanent loads and tide loads contribute to the overall response (weak axis bending), with tide loads contributing especially towards the ends of the bridge. In the lower part of the floating bridge the bridge girder is mostly governed by the ship collision ULS combination (both strong and weak axis bending is significant) and ULS 2 (mainly weak axis bending from traffic and permanent loads). With respect to the dynamic environmental response, the mean and dynamic wind load contribution is the governing load group for the strong axis moment, while the wind sea load contribution is the governing load group for the weak axis moment. The swell response only has a minor contribution to the response, and is significantly reduced in the coupled simulations due to aerodynamic damping.

For ALS, the 10-4 ship collision combinations have been found governing for the bridge girder. In the high part of the floating bridge the ship collision loads give rise to large weak axis bending moments which in the linear analysis give stress levels well above the yield limit, which indicate plastic response. Towards the north end, the ship collision loads give rise to large strong axis bending which also give stress levels well above the yield limit from the linear analysis. A detailed local nonlinear FEanalysis has been undertaken as part of the bridge to column local design work to assess the structural integrity for these collision loads and the stresses was considered to be manageable. In the north end it has also been recommended to study the structural integrity in the bridge girder further with a non-linear collapse analysis. The intact ALS combination with 10 000-year environmental loads is generally less severe than the intact ULS3 response when taking into account the difference in load factors (1.6 vs 1.0 for environmental loads).

For the columns connecting the pontoons to the bridge girder, environmental loads cause responses up to 50% of the ship impact loads. Hence, the columns are governed by ship impact alone with the exception of minor fatigue-prone details in the connection between the bridge girder and the column.

The mooring line responses are primarily driven by the lateral motion of the bridge girder, where the governing load groups are the dynamic and mean contribution from wind, however the thermal expansion of the bridge girder is also an important secondary driver to the response. The mooring lines response is non-linear, and as such the relevant static load effects have been included in the global analysis for mooring.

#### **6.2 Bridge girder**

The bridge girder is a closed orthotropic steel box girder like a state-of-the-art suspension bridge girder with optimal aerodynamic inclined outer webs. The total width of the box girder is 26.8 m, and the total height is 4m. A typical cross-section of the bridge girder is shown in the figure below.

_Figure 6-3: Typical cross-section at midspan for low part of bridge girder_

The major design changes in this FEED phase compared to the previous design phase 5 are the following:

- Shape of bridge girder has changed from rectangular to a standard suspension bridge box girder to reduce wind loads in drag and for a uniform appearance along the entire bridge.

- Fatigue design according to preliminary Eurocodes that results in increased plate thicknesses for top plates for the girder.

- Transverse and longitudinal bulkheads above the columns are increased in plate thickness to withstand a ship impact to the pontoons for a 10,000-year return period collision event.

The plate thicknesses for the top plate are 20mm in the slow lane, 18mm in the fast lane and 16mm in the bike path. Typical thicknesses of the bottom plate, lower and upper inclined plates vary from 14mm to 16mm in midspans and 20mm to 22mm above columns. The bridge girder towards the ends is additional strengthened.

<!-- side pdf=78 -->

The top deck plate and the lower inclined plates have troughs stiffeners. The troughs stiffeners are designed without openings at the transverse web plates, according to the latest state of art of fatigue design defined in preliminary Eurocode prNS-EN 1993-1-9 and CEN-TS-1993-1-901. These preliminary Eurocodes are approved for this project by the client NPRA. In the interface between the bridge girder and column, bulb stiffeners are preferable. The bottom deck plate has therefore longitudinal bulb stiffeners of typical sizes from BF 320x12 to BF 340x14.

The bridge girder is designed with diaphragms at typical every 4m. The bridge girder has transverse bulkheads above the columns. Elsewhere, the diaphragms are designed as a truss.

There are two longitudinal walls as trusses and bulkheads to reduce the shear lag effect. The walls are placed as shown in the next figure. Note that no longitudinal walls are placed in the midspan. The transverse and longitudinal bulkheads above the columns are designed to withstand a ship impact to the pontoons for a 10,000-year accidental load. The plate thicknesses for the bulkheads varies therefore from 25mm to 60mm.

The top plate has a crossfall of 3%. As stated in section 4.1.1 in the design brief [2], no extra outfitting for water drainage is implemented in this FEED phase.  This may be subject to further evaluation in a later phase of the project.

The steel quality of the plates and profiles of the bridge girder is S420M, except for a girder length of 60m towards the northern abutment where the steel quality is S460. The connecting four corners between the bridge girder and column is designed with casted steel pieces to increase the fatigue resistance of these vulnerable areas.

A typical span of the bridge girder is divided into three sections: midspan part of 70.4m length, two transition parts of 2x 16m length and a part above the column of 24m length. Longitudinal walls designed as bulkheads and trusses are included for the transition parts and the part above column to reduce the shear lag effect for these cross-sections.

##### **_6.2.1 Design methodology_**

The modelling and assumptions for the global analysis are described elsewhere. In this section, the given input to modelling and assumptions regarding the bridge girder for the floating bridge part are discussed.

The design of bridge girder cross sections is mainly based on results from the global analysis, with exception of the top deck where the design is mainly based on fatigue analysis.

The number of elements in the global analysis for the bridge girder span between the columns/pontoons is 16 with a typical element length of 7.925 m. Since the mesh of the global analysis model is coarse and not adjusted to the relevant subdivision of the span, it was decided to exclude the cross-section properties of the transition part in the global analysis and instead use only the other two cross-sections.

The chosen design methodology was to optimize the cross-sections (plate thicknesses and longitudinal stiffeners) with the non-commercial computer program “AAJPlatedSteelSections”, based on envelope force-set for extreme values with corresponding values for ULS2 and ULS3 along the bridge girder that was extracted from the global analysis.

However, according to section 8.1 in the design brief [2], the characteristic response from environmental actions shall be based on 90% fractile for ULS and 95% fractile for ALS, and not the envelope force-sets for extreme values. Therefore, the optimized cross-sections were finally checked with the correct design response for all limit states.

##### **_6.2.2 Shear lag_**

The effects of shear lag are calculated according to the design requirements given in NS-EN 1993-1-5.

According to clause 2.2(1)P in NS-EN 1993-1-5, the effects of shear lag on the stiffness of members shall be taken into account in the global analysis. However, according to clause 2.2(3) in NS-EN 19931-5, the effectives width of flanges should be taken as the lesser of the full width and L/8 per side of the web, where L is the span. Since is 2 ∗ 126.8 𝑚 /8=31.7 𝑚 larger than the full width of the largest plate, the top plate of 22.8m, the full width can be applied as effective width of flanges. Hence the effects of shear lag can be ignored for the stiffness of members in the global analysis. It has been shown that the dynamic modes are not significantly affected by shear lag, see documentation in memo 10205546-13-NOT-334.

At serviceability and fatigue limit state, an effectives width for elastic shear lag is used in accordance with clause 3.2 of NS-EN 1993-1-5. The effectives width is beff = β b0, where the effectives width factor β is calculated based on formulas given in table 3.1 of the Eurocode. At ultimate and accidental limit state, elastic-plastic shear lag effects are considered as given in clause 3.3(1)c). As the National Annex does not specify a preferred method, the recommended method in NOTE 3 is adapted. The effective width is beff = β<sup>κ</sup> b0, where the factors β and κ are calculated according to table 3.1 of NS-EN 1993-1-5. The dynamic modes are not significantly affected by the shear lag, see Appendix K.

The shear lag reduction factor for all the different plate widths of the bridge girder for all different locations along the span are calculated, see Appendix K. Typical shear lag reduction factors are 0.97 for ULS and 0.70 for FLS

##### **_6.2.6 Fatigue_**

Fatigue regulations is facing a change based on observations and research in central Europe. Use of these preliminary regulations is in agreement with the client NPRA and considered appropriate for Bjørnafjorden bridge in this FEED phase. The same preliminary regulations are also applied in current major bridge design projects in Norway like Julsundet suspension bridge and Langenuen suspension bridge.

Fatigue calculations have been performed in accordance with the following two preliminary Eurocode:

- prNS-EN-1993-1-9 Eurocode 3: Design of steel structures. Part 1-9: Fatigue. Published: 2023-03-09

- CEN/TC 1993-1-901 Fatigue design of orthotropic bridge decks with the hot spot stress method. Document date 2021-05-20

The following critical areas are checked:

- Traffic lane details at midspan and above column for both slow and fast lane

- Connection between bridge girder and column

From the traffic lane results, details C3c and C4a has an insufficient fatigue life of 47 and 88 years, and local optimization may be necessary for these details. Also, the following details can be pointed out as the most critical:

- Detail C1c: The fatigue life is approximately the same in the mid span of the bridge and above columns, and the detail has the largest fatigue damage in the slow lane.

- Details C3c: The details in connection with the weld between stiffener and bulkhead above columns have a relatively low fatigue life. In particular detail C3cII has an insufficient fatigue life of 47 years and local optimization may be necessary. See further explanation below.

- Details C4ac: The details in connection with the weld between stiffener and bulkhead above columns have a relatively low fatigue life. In particular detail C4aI has an insufficient fatigue life of 88 years and local optimization may be necessary. See further explanation below.

- Detail C8a: The stiffener splice joints also have a relatively large fatigue damage, especially in the slow lane.

It should be noted that for Detail C3c there is some conservatism in the global effects since global stress at the top plate and not at stiffener bottom is used in the calculations. Also, not including the weld in the local model is expected to be conservative.

The stress for Detail C3c is mainly a result of stiffener and top plate acting as a beam between transverse frames or walls. Hence, there are several measures that can effectively reduce fatigue stresses. Increased stiffener and top plate cross-section will naturally reduce the stress. Also, reduced span length and more flexible supports at the transverse stiffening will reduce the reaction moment and thereby reduce the stress. For Detail C4aI, the stress is in the web of the transverse frame, and the fatigue life of 88 years is obtained when a location close to the walls of the column inside the girder is considered. Increasing the transverse web thickness at the columns should increase the fatigue life. Having sufficient fatigue life for the midspan cross-section F1, the required measures for both critical details should amount to local optimizations around the columns.

For the connection between bridge girder and column, sufficient fatigue life is obtained for all the selected details. Comparing the results at axis 9 and axis 37, it is seen that there can be a rather large variation in fatigue life at the different columns, and it is expected that for most of the columns the fatigue damage is significantly lower than for axis 37, where the fatigue life is close to 100 years.

#### **6.3 Pontoons**

The pontoon has two main functions; to provide buoyancy and stability to the bridge, and to act as a collision buffer to achieve a predictable response in the bridge in the event of a ship impact. The pontoon will within reasonable limits always be weaker than a striking ship, and the design is thus optimized to reduce the direct and indirect consequences of an impact event rather than to avoid damage.

In this design phase, a new design criterion has been introduced; a ship impact scenario with a 100year return period. This is in addition to a ship impact scenario of a 10,000-year return period. Due to these design conditions, the major design changes compared to previous design phases are the following:

- The number of watertight compartments in the pontoon is increased to limit the loss of buoyancy and flooded volumes.

- The outer compartments of the pontoon are designed with corrugated bulkheads to absorb more energy during a ship impact.

_Figure 6-14 Plan view of watertight compartments, showing permanent ballast tanks in blue._

Pontoon type 1 is the most common pontoon with a draught of 5.3 m and no mooring connections. Pontoon type 2 and type 3 has two and four moorings with fairlead connection, and therefore increased draught to compensate for the downward force from the moorings and to move the fairlead further away from a potential ship impact from a bulbous bow. The areas close to the fairlead connection for these two pontoon types are strengthened to withstand a mooring breaking load. This has resulted in a decrease in internal compartment size (smaller waterproof tanks) compared to phase 5 of the project, as well as corrugated plates in the bulkheads towards the outer shell to reduce stiff points that could cause high impact loads. However, the plate thicknesses and stiffener sizes are optimized towards the conventional loads that a normal pontoon would encounter. The pontoon structural layout is thus inspired by ship impact response but dimensioned according to conventional loads.

#### **6.4 Columns**

The columns connect the bridge girder with the pontoons. In this phase, the columns are designed with the same outer shape for both the high and low columns.

A rectangular cross-section of 8.4m x 8.0m is used in the top part of the column for easy integration into the bridge girder structure. A four-meter-high transition piece converts the rectangular section into an octagonal section to allow for improved aerodynamic properties while maintaining an easily fabricated structure. This octagonal cross-section is then kept all the way down to the pontoon, in which it is integrated directly into the pontoon structure.

The height of the columns varies from 43.9m at axis 3 to 5.8m at axis 39. The most used height is 10.5m, valid for axes 16-35. The columns at axes 3-8 are strengthened compared to the columns at axes 9-39.

For the columns at axes 3 – 8, the outer shell plate is 45mm for the upper part, 40mm for the center part and 35mm for the lower part of the column. For the other columns at axes 9-39, the outer shell plate is 40mm along the whole length. For all columns, the stringers vary in thickness from 10mm to 40mm and the stiffener size is BF 400x14.

<!-- side pdf=91 -->

The steel quality of outer shell plating is S460 to resist the torsional loads with the least amount of steel. The steel quality for the internal frames is S420, while the stiffeners is S355 steel. The connecting four corners between the column and bridge girder and the connecting eight corners between the column and pontoon is designed with casted steel pieces to increase the fatigue resistance of these vulnerable areas.

_Figure 6-17 Cross-section of the columns, with top section (left) and the lower sections (right)._

The steel columns between the floating pontoons and the bridge superstructure will experience very high forces in the event of a ship collision. The section forces from the 10 000-year accidental load significantly exceed those from ULS and ALS weather events. Section forces from the 10 000-year accidental load without load factors are also larger than the 100-year accidental loading including load factors.

<!-- side pdf=92 -->

Repair of the columns is deemed very challenging, especially for the higher columns. Hence, the columns are designed to have negligible damage even after a 10-000-year return period collision event, which is a somewhat more conservative approach than that in the design basis [1]. For the column to withstand the high forces due to a ship collision, the connection within the bridge girder must withstand the same forces. Therefore, the longitudinal and transverse bulkheads which are an extension of the column within the bridge girder are designed accordingly.

It should be notated that to fulfil this requirement of negligible damage, the capacity of the columns and the connection within the bridge girder and thereby the amount of steel are increased compared to what is necessary to withstand the static loads from the global analysis.

The highest columns are strengthened to withstand both a high torsion and bending moment simultaneously, whereas the lower columns are primarily dimensioned by torsional loads. However, the height of the highest columns allows for more flexibility in torsion prior to reaching yield stress, and the total weight pr. m column is thus somewhat less than for the lowest columns. The transition between high and low column types is taken northwards of axis A8.

The required plate thickness of the different parts of the column are determined from quasi-static and transient analysis. The response is based on the column loads from the Stage 3 global impact simulations. This was again based on a pontoon design prior to final weight-optimization. After this optimization, the local impact loads from pontoon impacts were reduced, and the corresponding column loads will likely be reduced. Hence, the simulation results herein are somewhat conservative.

To capture the behavior of the different column heights, capacity design is performed both for the maximum column height in axis A3 and the typical height in axis A24. The lessons learned from these two columns were then used to dimension the remaining column heights.

Both columns are evaluated using two separate models each. One where parts of the superstructure are included to evaluate the connection detail and get the correct boundary condition, and another using only a rigid support at the top edge to reduce runtime for the analysis where the superstructure is not required.

The column design and the selected thickness of the column skin plates has been verified through several analyses. The columns capacity to withstand combined torsional and principal bending moment was found and plotted. In addition, the expected dynamic load was applied to two different columns before the damage was assessed. The thickness of the column surface plates have been optimized under the assumption that damage from the 10 000-year accidental load should be limited to small areas in yield. The internal stiffeners are not as carefully selected through iterations and could potentially be adjusted in the detailed design.

Through several analysis it was verified that the capacity of the column connection detail is sufficient to withstand the impact loadings in both axis A24 and A3. However, the stresses and utilization in axis A3 is quite high.

#### **7.1 Introduction**

The cable stayed bridge is constituting the south portion of the Bjørnafjorden Bridge, "cantilevering" from Reksteren across the Svarvhelleholmen Island and connecting to the floating bridge at axis 3. The tower is positioned on the island, while the 430 m main span allows the navigation to pass under at a height of 45 m and a width of 250 m or more. The bridge spans from the south abutment to the tower are 54 + 3 x 74 + 144 m = 420 m. The bridge deck is in principle divided into a steel portion above water, and concrete above land continuing with the concrete viaduct spans. The cable stayed bridge is of a fully conventional type, and the most significant influence from the floating bridge is in general tidal variation and to some extent wave motion of pontoon at axis 3. #### **7.2 Concept for cable stayed bridge and south abutment**

Of the 430 m long main span, 395.2 m belongs to the cable stayed bridge, whereas the remaining 34.8 m is part of the floating bridge. With the 144 m side span there is a total of 533.2 m closed steel box girder within the cable stayed bridge. The remainder of bridge deck consisting of 154 m cable stayed side span and three viaducts spans, all together 128 m, is a closed concrete box structure of total length 282 m. The tower, piers and abutment are concrete constructions.

The bridge deck and tower have a tight fit in transverse direction where the deck is supported on normal sliding bearings to the tower legs allowing only longitudinal movements of the deck. The tower therefore becomes a strong point being able to accommodate the high transverse loading from ship collision and normal ULS load situations. The bridge superstructure is furthermore integrated with the abutment and the piers and the ship collision impact and ULS load combinations therefore also lead to high lateral loads to the side span piers.

The thermal contraction/expansion of the viaduct spans results in flexure of the piers. In general, the aim has been to minimize number of bridge bearings and to avoid expansion joints in the bridge deck, neither at the abutments, at the tower nor at the intersection to the floating bridge. Axial compression forces in the bridge deck caused by stay inclinations are in balance about the tower, whereas other axial loads are transmitted through the cable stayed bridge to the abutment.

At the tower section of the bridge deck, the ULS strong axis bending moment becomes 4270 MNm, while the ALS ship collisions to the pontoons or deck house impacts to the floating bridge deck structure leads to a slightly smaller bending moment of 4080 MNm.

From above is seen that the most severe impact from the floating bridge is the environmental load closely followed by ship collision to the bridge deck and the first pontoon of the floating bridge. These load situations are however fully common design situations for a cable stayed bridge which therefore is built using well-known standard solutions not adding significant risks to the project.

Construction of the bridge girder for the selected concept can be done by movable scaffolding system (MSS) spanning from pier to pier, incremental launching (ILM) or cast in place using traditional scaffolding supported on ground.

The concrete girder of the cable stayed bridge can be seen to consist of well documented standard products not adding significant risks to the project.

#### **7.5 Bridge girder joint**

The joint between the steel bridge girder and the concrete girder is located 138 m from the tower near the first side span pier. Here the bending moments are relatively moderate in comparison to the section at the tower and the connection by means of post-tensioning cables and high-strength bars is then less demanding.

The joint will be based on the same principles as used for the Pont de Normandie in France. The posttensioning cables will be anchored a certain distance from the joint inside the steel box and be cast in the concrete deck ensuring that no tension occurs even in ULS load combination between the 2 sections. Along the circumference of the steel box a number of high strength steel rods will be installed at tight spacings ensuring that no separation will occur nor for the local loading.

The connection could be established in two steps. First the steel box and the concrete deck are brought near to each other and partially stressed. In this locked situation a stich joint concrete (grout) is cast ensuring the complete connection of the two bridge deck types. After curing of the stich joint, the cables and bars are fully tensioned. The bridge girder joint of the cable stayed bridge is not a standard solution but is however well-known technology from other large cable stayed bridges as the Pont de Normandie in France, the Russky Bridge in Russia and Stonecutters Bridge in Hong Kong.

#### **7.6 Tower**

The tower has a distinct A-shape that is architecturally preferred, and which is optimal seen in relation to the demanding ship collision loads and environmental loads being able to accommodate large forces in the transverse direction.

The tower is a concrete structure 223 m tall and A- shaped, meaning it has a high transverse stiffness and strength. The tower will also act strong for uneven loading of the cable planes in traffic and ship collision situations due to the interconnection of the legs at the top. The lower legs and cross beams are strong and forms a frame able to transfer large horizontal loads from ship impact to the ground. The form is technically sound and aligns well with the architect's preference, see Figure 7-6.

The lower legs taper towards the foundation, where the bending moments therefore become small, hence the leg sections need to be large where they meet the cross beams. Due to esthetical reason the tower foundations are selected fully buried into the ground.

The analyses show that ship collisions and ULS tower demands are almost equally important.

The concrete volumes at the crossbeam connection to the leg are quite substantial and will occupy some space inside the tower. The frame connection requires transverse posttension from outside leg to outside leg through the cross beam, and likely also vertical posttensioning some depth down the inner flange of the lower leg.

Above the bridge deck, the tower legs are slender due to aesthetic reasons meaning that the compartment becomes compact. Moreover, the anchorages have been placed asymmetric in the legs making it possible to position all the stay anchorages along a straight-line meeting each other at the tower top. At the location of steel boxes for the cable stay anchorages in upper legs of tower, there is only limited space for a stair to pass on one side of the asymmetrical placed stay anchorages. #### **7.7 Cable stays**

The cable stays are multi-strand cables having 42 strands nearest the tower, each 15.7 mm in diameter increasing up to 88 strands for the longest stays. The wire strength is 1860 MPa and hot dip galvanized and PE-sheathed individually and enclosed in HDPE-pipes. The cables have anchorages that fit with the steel tubes integrated with the main structure at either end. The stays are constructed by strand-by-strand tensioned from the tower.

The stays are arranged in 4 fans, each containing 16 cables, the shortest nearest to the tower are subjected to the lowest loads, whereas the flattest outermost cables have the highest loads. The cables outbalance each other horizontally at the tower anchorage, however minor deviations are acceptable, and forces may be slightly redistributed.

<!-- side pdf=100 -->

The cable stay stressing ends are anchored in steel boxes in the tower legs making the tensioning process easy, whereas their passive ends are anchored at the bottom end of steel tubes sticking trough the verges of the bridge deck. Dampers are furthermore provided at the upper part of the tubes above deck level.

All load effects are comparable with those of other cable stayed bridges, except wave load effects to the longest stays nearest to the first pontoon of the floating bridge.

The cable capacity for fatigue is checked and found to be very high. The fatigue analysis shows that the environmental and tide load represent the governing loads for the longest stays while traffic load governs the shortest stays.

Additional damping might be necessary for the longest stays if snow is expected to accumulate on the stays. Other bridges have additional damping installed to safeguard against stay oscillations due to possible snow accumulation, also well known by the Joint Venture.

The stays constitute an important part of the cable stayed bridge, however, they are welldocumented standard products that are not supposed to add a significant risk to the project. The total quantity is 1250 tonnes approximately.

They are 10 m wide at the top and have an inclination of 1:50 in the transversal direction. The width is 2.5 m. The pier in axis 1D is hollow to reduce stiffness. The height varies in accordance with the terrain, and the tallest pier is about 53 m. The piers will be governed by the ship collision effects (ULS and ALS) and forces from the load combination ULS3 (environmental load dominant). #### **8.1 General on mooring system**

A mooring system to support the floating bridge concept for crossing Bjørnafjorden has been further developed and analyzed in this FEED phase.

The curved bridge has 37 pontoons with 126.8 m center spacing between each pontoon. Mooring lines will be connected to five pontoons relatively close to the center, where the pontoon at axis 19 has four lines and the other moored pontoons have two lines connected to each. This gives a total of 12 mooring lines and anchors for the mooring system. All anchors are suction anchors located on a relatively flat seabed with sufficient seabed soil for installation and stability. The pontoons and mooring lines are presented in the figure below.

_Figure 8-1 Bridge pontoons and mooring system overview._

All components of the mooring system are well known to the offshore industry. Dual axis fairlead with chain stoppers are installed on the moored pontoons. The fairleads will reduce any chain bending moments on the top end of the mooring chain. The fairleads are installed close to the bottom of the moored pontoons. This is done to reduce corrosion on the top end mooring chain and to avoid damage from ship collision.

The mooring lines have 147 mm R4 chain and 124 mm coated steel wire ropes. All lines have 50 m top chain between fairlead connection on the pontoon and the wire rope segment. All lines have 100 m bottom chain between the wire rope segment and the anchor. Mooring line pretension has been optimized to give the desired line pretension or lateral restoring stiffness for each pontoon. The mooring line tension will also provide minimal total enforced loads from the mooring lines on each pontoon during equilibrium bridge position in calm weather. The mooring lines are pretensioned to 1.7-2.5 MN, providing a relatively taut system where the bottom chain will be suspended and not come in contact with the seabed. All mooring lines consist of five main components based on a standard composition for a semi-taut mooring system:

- Pontoon connection, chain stopper/fairlead

- Top chain (between fairlead on pontoons and wire top end)

- Sheathed/coated steel wire rope

- Bottom chain (between wire bottom and anchor pad eye)

- Anchor (suction type)

#### **8.3 Geotechnical evaluation**

Bjørnafjorden is characterized by steep flanks with slope angles exceeding 30°, surrounding the essentially flat central fjord basin. In the shallower areas, the terrain is hummocky due to bedrock outcrops. However, several tens of meters of sediment have accumulated in the basin and significant sediment thicknesses are encountered even in slopes with more than 20° inclination. The soil conditions at the site are primarily the result of the glacial processes and the sediments consist of soft clays.

Several anchor types have previously been evaluated for mooring of the floating bridge concepts. The suction anchor is considered the most suitable due to its proven technology, its ability to sustain high design loads and its flexibility to be designed to accommodate the varying sediment thicknesses in the fjord.

Since the pre-FEED, supplementary soil investigations are conducted and sub bottom profiler data at anchor locations are gathered. An update of the pre-FEED activities as presented in 2019 with the newly acquired information is presented. In addition, a structural assessment of boulder encounter is given. Calculations of geohazard aspects as well as holding capacity performed assumes a low estimate shear strength profile for the larger fjord basin which is on the conservative side.

The current anchor locations were optimized based on the geohazard study from 2017. Anchor placement is a trade-off between optimal anchor placement and the risk of loss of anchors and its consequence for the global bridge response. When applying this philosophy in the FEED study we see a significant improvement in the global bridge response. We also see a robust bridge concept that can cope with the project safety requirements regarding loss of several anchors.

A geohazard study is performed to identify slopes that could potentially fail and impact proposed anchor locations. The current geohazard analysis is an update of the study conducted previously using newly available geotechnical data, geophysical data, earthquake loading estimates, and anchor locations. The analyses are conducted for static conditions with unknown triggering conditions and for earthquakes with return periods of 2750-years and 10000-years. The following tasks are performed as part of the geohazard study: 1D infinite static and pseudo-static slope stability analyses, 1D nonlinear seismic slope stability analyses, 2D static and pseudo-static Finite Element analyses, assessment of failure volumes from 1D and 2D analyses, quasi-3D landslide runout simulations and calculation of impact forces for the anchors based on the runout simulations.

A total of 18 slope stability profiles are analyzed in PLAXIS. Some of these profiles are subdivided so that each analysis only includes one slope section. In total 27 2D stability sections are run. The 2D slope stability calculations revealed that only three of the static analyses meet the required factor of safety of 1.4. The 2D static and pseudo-static analyses only provide factors of safety against slope failure. They do not provide any information about what would happen when a slope does fail. Therefore, the objective of the landslide/ debris flow runout analyses is to evaluate if any of the critical slopes identified in the 2D slope stability analyses could impact bridge anchor locations if they were to fail, and what their velocity and height would be at the time of impact.

The landslide runout analyses are performed for 27 failure volumes based on the results of the 1D and 2D slope stability analyses. The maximum failure volume is for slide 19, on the northern flank of the fjord, and is about 2 million m<sup>3</sup> . This slide also has the maximum flow height and maximum velocity from all runout analyses. Anchors 3, 4, 9, 10 and 12 at base case locations are not impacted by any of the simulated landslides. Anchors 5, 6, 7 and 8 at base case locations are impacted by multiple different simulated landslides. Impact loads on anchors are estimated based on the results from the simulations. These are calculated assuming either that the landslide rides on top of the

<!-- side pdf=118 -->

seabed or with a ploughing depth of 5 or 10 m. The largest impact loads for all three assumptions are for anchors 8 and 11 due to slide 19.

The base case anchor positions were selected at an early stage of the FEED study, before the major part of the geohazard assessment was conducted. Anchor positioning has been selected primarily to improve the mooring layout for the global bridge response and to a lesser extent to minimize the geotechnical risks of landslide mechanisms impacting the anchors. Finding the optimal anchor position is a compromise between the global bridge response and finding seabed locations that minimize geotechnical risk and cost of the anchor.

Holding capacity calculations for the suction anchors were calculated using the NGI program BIFURC. Operational loads have been considered and resulted in anchor diameters of Ø6 m for 9 out of 12 positions. The total skirt length of these 9 anchors is between 16-20 m. For anchor positions 9, 10 and 12 larger diameter suction anchors are needed to cope with the operational loads due to limited sediment thickness. Anchor positions 10 and 12 are suggested to have Ø10 m diameter with total lengths of 13 and 15 m respectively. For anchor location no. 9 the base case/ nominal position needs further consideration due to limited sediment thickness and relatively large loads. #### **9.7 Cable stayed bridge cables – vibration and damping.**

##### **_9.7.1 Cable vibrations due to wind._**

Since the mid 1980'ies it has been recognized that long span cable stayed bridges worldwide has encountered cable vibrations excited by aerodynamic forces developing in medium to high winds combined with rainfall snowfall or ice accumulation. The cable vibrations developing under such circumstances may reach amplitudes of several times the cable diameter and are unacceptable from a point of view of user discomfort or structural integrity. It follows that cable stayed bridges needs some sort of mitigation measures which may include mechanical dampers, stay cable surface treatment (helical fillets or dimples) and cross ties. In many cases hydraulic dampers is the chosen device for mitigation of stay cable vibrations sometimes aided by cable surface modification.

Appendix E shows details regarding calculation of necessary damping to mitigate rain-wind vibrations and ice-snow galloping. Requirements described by the Post Tensioning Institute is used and the proposed design fulfil PTI requirements and the requirements in the Design basis, including a minimum damping of 4% of critical. This require dampers that are placed internally in the post tensioning tube for the shortest hangers, and external for the longest, as outlined in Figure 9-11. The proposed dampers are commercially available.

##### **_9.7.2 Parametric excitation_**

Parametric excitation of the stay cables is addressed by studying the longest cable. The parameter driving the parametric excitation is assumed as the axial force of the cable; this will in turn vary the geometric stiffness of it, which can cause an unstable state.

#### **10.8 Environmental loading conditions**

##### **_10.8.1 Environmental screening_**

To select the environmental conditions for the combined simulations of windsea, swell and wind extreme conditions, a selection process has been established. The selection process comprises of an individual evaluation of the windsea, swell and wind, where for each load group the governing load cases from West and from East are selected for combined simulation.

As a practical measure to reduce the complexity of selecting load cases and reducing the number of combinations, it is chosen to assume that the worst wind condition can be combined from the worst condition from swell and windsea for each sector (West and East). This means that for the West sector (180 – 360 degrees) the worst wind direction from West is combined with all the selected swell and windsea cases in this sector. And for the East sector (0 – 180 degrees) the worst wind direction from East is combined with the selected windsea cases in this sector (no swell from East according to Metocean Specification [4]).

- Forces, motion and moments in all points along the bridge girder

- Axial force in cable stays

- Forces and moments in all tower legs and crown

- Tension in mooring lines

- Forces and moments in piers

- Forces and moments in columns

##### **_10.9.1 Combination method_**

##### **_10.9.1.1 General_**

A direct approach has been taken for the load combinations in order to account for correlation between:

1. Individual load groups

2. individual force components

This means that load groups analyzed by dynamic analysis (wind waves, swells and wind) are analyzed simultaneously in coupled time domain analysis. This preserves the correlation between responses from dynamic load groups.

The correlation between individual response variables is preserved by performing the load combination using the full timeseries for the dynamic response, effectively ending up with timeseries of the combined design responses. The time series for individual response variables can then be used for e.g. stress calculations with full correlation between individual force components.

##### **_10.9.1.2 Combination of static load groups_**

Load groups analyzed by means of static analysis include permanent, temperature, traffic, tide and current loads. The first step in the combinations is to combine all static results into a single group with load cases representing the minimum and maximum values for each response variable. When combining forces this results in 12 combined static cases. Appropriate load factors are applied to each group.

<!-- side pdf=147 -->

##### **_10.9.1.3 Combination of dynamic load groups_**

Prior to combination the dynamic responses are split into its mean and dynamic parts. The number of dynamic load cases to be used in the load combinations are determined from the wave and wind screenings.

Each dynamic load case is then combined with each static load group, resulting in a total of Nstatic cases * Ndynamic cases unique combination cases. For example, with 9 dynamic load cases the unique number of combination cases is 12 * 9 = 108.

Each dynamic load case has 10 seed realizations, and the combinations are performed for each seed, resulting in Nstatic cases * Ndynamic cases * Nseeds number of combination cases.

##### **_10.9.2 Extreme value estimation_**

Extreme value estimation is done by fitting the location and scale parameters of a right skewed Gumbel distribution based on the single observed maximum value from each seed. The extreme maximum value for a given percentile is then calculated from the fitted distribution. Both maximum and minimum (largest negative) values are estimated with this approach.

Individual forces and motions are reported with their estimated extreme value for the percentiles given above in Table 5-1.

The long-term 100y windsea response analysis has also been performed, see Appendix G. This analysis show that for most of the response the short term 90 percentile response estimates are conservative as compared to the expected 100y long term response. This is when the design is based on the extreme sea states as reported in the MOS. However, this is a topic which we recommend should be investigating further based on a larger amount of hindcast data than the 15 year available at the moment.

##### **_10.9.3 Design force envelopes_**

Design values of section forces are also made in envelope format. These are used for design checks where it is impractical to use the full time series of the design forces.

The envelopes consist of a total of 12 cases where each case represents the maximum or minimum observed value of individual force components across all combination cases and all seeds. For each force component the associated other force components are taken from the same time instance.

This means that the envelopes represent snapshots into the time realizations ensuring full correlation between individual force components. The force envelopes are hence actual observed values and not based on extreme value estimation.

## **11 Hydrodynamics**

Hydrodynamic effects that are of particular interest and that are not straightforward to implement in a global analysis model are addressed in Appendix H (where technical notes with exhaustive background information are attached as enclosures). The effects and the work related to them are briefly described below.

#### **11.1 Wave-current interaction**

The interaction with a current modifies the wavelength of a propagating wave for an observer moving with the wave’s phase velocity, or the wave frequency for an observer fixed in space. This interaction modifies the wave loads on a surface piercing marine structure, such as the Bjørnafjorden Bridge’s pontoons. The effect on the second-order drift loads is often denoted as wave-drift damping, which can be approximated from the wave drift load transfer functions in OrcaFlex. The work therefore concentrates on the effect that the wave-current interaction has on the linear wave excitation loads.

Model tests performed at SINTEF Ocean in 2020-21 suggested that the wave-current interaction effect on wave excitation loads could lead to a 40% increase in the weak-axis moment for the tested conditions. However, it is believed that empirically increasing the weak-axis moment with 40% could lead to an over-conservative design, amongst others because the tests were performed in longcrested sea states whereas realistic wind-generated sea states at the site have significant wave spreading. Therefore, two methods to approximate the wave-current interaction effects in the OrcaFlex model were investigated:

- 1) Changing the description of the incident wave phase by modifying the dispersion relation. This effect is not available in OrcaFlex and was therefore studied using the self-developed multimodal frequency-domain solver WAWI. If to be included in OrcaFlex, it would involve pre-generation of wave excitation load time series to be enforced in the simulations.

- 2) Modifying the frequency-axis of the wave excitation load transfer functions through a Doppler shift together with an empirically founded increase of 10% in the horizontal wave excitation forces.

The latter approach is straightforward to implement in time domain simulation software such as OrcaFlex. By choosing this approach over modification of the dispersion relation, the modified correlation of the excitation loads on different pontoons is not accounted for in the analysis. However, as this effect is found to have limited relevance in the site-specific wind-generated sea states, the method 2) where the wave excitation loads are modified was recommended to be used in global analysis. It is noted, however, that further studies may be relevant to address swell waves that are more long-crested by nature.

#### **11.2 Inhomogeneous sea states**

The Bjørnafjorden Bridge spans over a large spatial area, and as documented in the MetOcean specification (MOS), the sea state parameters are non-homogeneous across the strait. The inhomogeneity is described by spatial variations in the significant wave height 𝐻𝑠 , the spectral peak period 𝑇𝑝 and the mean wave direction 𝜃0 . The parameter variation for sea states defined in groups C1 and C2 (which are thought to be governing for many responses) is illustrated in Figure 11-1.

<!-- side pdf=162 -->

_Figure 11-1 Distribution of sea state parameters for wind seas in classification groups C1 and C2._

It is natural to assume that the significant variation in sea state parameters influences the global response of the bridge. However, unless restricting ourselves to the variation in 𝐻𝑠 , such effects are not straightforward to implement in the global OrcaFlex analysis model. Three approaches are studied to better understand the importance of sea state inhomogeneity:

- 1) Using the multimodal frequency domain solver WAWI where the inhomogeneous wave fields are consistently described.

- 2) Enforcing pre-generated wave excitation load time histories for each pontoon in OrcaFlex, where the inhomogeneity is accounted for in the generation of the individual time histories.

- 3) Modifying (scaling) wave excitation load transfer functions individually for each pontoon according to the local 𝐻𝑠 value.

The analysis suggests that accounting for inhomogeneous sea state parameters, compared to using homogeneous sea states with parameters taken in the middle of the fjord, tends to reduce the dimensioning weak-axis moments. Neglecting inhomogeneity is therefore deemed conservative.

<!-- side pdf=163 -->

#### **11.3 Second-order hydrodynamic loads on pontoons**

Slowly varying wave drift loads are second-order hydrodynamic properties that can be approximated efficiently in OrcaFlex using Newman’s approximation. Alternatively, they can also be described through full quadratic transfer functions (QTF), but at a much higher computational cost (meaning slower simulations). Examples of full QTFs are shown in Figure 11-2. By comparison, Newman’s approximation assumes that the relevant parts of the QTFs can be estimated sufficiently well from their main diagonals, which corresponds to the mean wave drift loads.

_Figure 11-2 QTFs for inline slowly varying wave drift force parallel to bridge girder (left) and pontoon axis (right), respectively._

The analysis suggests that using Newman’s approximation in the global analysis model gives sufficiently accurate loads on individual pontoons. However, Newman’s approximation does not properly account for phase differences between loads on different pontoons and can as such account for possible correlated excitation of global resonances. To investigate this, a model with three adjacent pontoons was considered using full QTFs, suggesting that the risk of correlated excitation is little as long as the waves are not long-crested. Nevertheless, a methodology to perform sensitivity studies for the entire global bridge model was proposed to further mitigate this risk element as possible future work.

The studies also emphasized the following:

- Hydrodynamic interaction effects between pontoons are significant for many wave directions and should be duly accounted for whether Newman’s approximation or full QTFs are used.

- Rigid body motions significantly influence the wave drift loads and should be accounted for in future work.

It is, however, important to keep in mind that slowly varying wave drift loads are second-order quantities that are quadratic in the wave amplitude of the incident waves. Considering the moderate wave heights in Bjørnafjorden, these loads are therefore not expected to be important for the design unless they induce resonant response that cannot be excited directly either by linear wave or wind loads.

Computational fluid dynamics (CFD) simulations were performed for a restricted number of cases for a single fixed pontoon (Figure 11-3).

## **12 Ship collision**

This section highlights the global ship collision assessment. For details on global and local ship collision response see Appendix J.

#### **12.1 Summary**

The ship collision appendix J describes status of ship collisions analysis in the concept development work of a floating bridge over Bjørnafjorden during the FEED phase of the project. Both global and local effects are considered. LS-DYNA is used as the primary tool to study the local ship impact process, whereas OrcaFlex is used for the global bridge response. The same global bridge model is used for ship impact as for ULS simulations, but with a few additional elements to incorporate the ship impact load.

The local response found in LS-DYNA is used as input for a nonlinear relation between the ship movement and the contact force applied to the bridge in a way that dissipates local deformation energy. OrcaFlex then handles the dynamic bridge response including the various damping mechanisms and controls the 6-DOF motion of the striking vessel including the frequency-dependent added mass. The results of the global collision simulations can then be used to assess the resulting damage level to the pontoon and bridge girder (from the local analysis, how much energy should be dissipated for the various impact scenarios along the bridge), and to get sectional forces in column, bridge girder and tower to be used for capacity assessments.

The design basis [1] lists the relevant ship impact scenarios, which are based on the assumption of a Traffic Separation Scheme (TSS) in order to lower the risk of impacts to the bridge. It is separated between impact events with a 10 000-year return period (as a typical accidental limit state) and a 100-year return period (as a measure to ensure repairability for more frequent events). The 10 000year impact is considered as an accidental limit state event, whereas the 100-year return period impacts are considered as a separate ULS event with specific load factors given in [1] . For the accidental limit state, damage can be allowed in all structural elements if this does not lead to progressive collapse of the bridge in a 100-year storm without load factors. For the 100-year event, [1] specifies different acceptance criteria for different load factors and allows local damage in the impact zone. As a robustness measure the columns are strengthened to not undergo large plastic deformation for both 100- and 10 000-year impact events. Hence, the major damage from a ship impact event will be local plastic deformation in the vicinity of the impact location, and not damage as the impact force travels along the other bridge components.

From a global response perspective, the dominant effect of an impact event will be loss of buoyancy and some loss of waterplane stiffness as one or more compartments in the pontoon are flooded. If traffic is to be allowed on the bridge in a post-impact phase, the bridge would have to withstand the conventional ULS2 and ULS3 loads including load factors. Loss of buoyancy may introduce considerable weak-axis moments in the bridge girder close to the pontoon, and this may in combination with traffic and wave loading lead to an overutilization of the girder capacity (if the flooded volume is large). Two measures are included to mitigate this: First, the size of the internal compartments in the pontoon has been reduced so that less buoyancy is lost pr. flooded tank. Second, the available permanent water ballast in each pontoon has been increased so that water can be removed from the ballast tanks to restore the buoyancy and in part the trim of the pontoon. With these measures, the long-term consequence of a pontoon impact event is in large mitigated. By removing water-ballast similar to the flooded volume the global effect on the bridge girder is removed, and the bridge can withstand the ULS conditions including load factors. However, local repairs in the vicinity of the damaged area are to be expected.

<!-- side pdf=166 -->

The pontoon structure is optimized towards ULS loads, and not to withstand ship impacts. Hence, the primary function of the pontoon is to provide buoyancy and stability to the bridge, and to act as an impact buffer that controls the impact load transferred from the ship to the other parts of the bridge structure (like an energy absorber on a car). The pontoon structure undergoes significant deformation during a bow impact. For the 100-year events, two-compartment flooding is a likely result. For the 10 000-year events, four and perhaps five-compartment damage may occur.

The bridge cross-sections are not dimensioned to withstand impact loads, but the choice of a structural wind nose is beneficial in two aspects: it moves the impact location further away from the traffic lanes and it provides significant resistance to induce damage on the impacting object. 100year impacts from a deckhouse structure results in minor plate dents, whereas 10 000-year impacts may cause collapse of the wind nose for the typical mid-span cross-section used throughout the bridge girder.

#### **12.6 Sensitivity studies**

A range of sensitivity studies were performed as part of the FEED phase. A summary is given in the following.

##### **_12.6.1 Local response - mesh size_**

Three mesh sizes were evaluated for the pontoon structure, using the v9 pontoon model with ULSoptimized structure. Three mesh sizes were evaluated:

- A coarse mesh with 160 mm element length in the shell plating, two elements over the stiffener height. This is typically considered too coarse both for the bending stiffness of the stiffener and the fracture prediction.

- A medium mesh with 100mm element length in the shell plating, four elements over the stiffener height. This is typically considered adequate for the stiffener and within the valid region of the fracture criterion.

- A refined mesh with 80mm element length in the shell plating, five elements over the stiffener height. This is typically considered very good for the stiffener and within the valid region of the fracture criterion.

The three pontoon meshes were impacted by a rigid supply bow and a container bow at two locations on the pontoon hull. The results show that the two finest mesh sizes converge on a forcevs-displacement level, but that the most refined mesh shows the most realistic detail in the fracture propagation. Hence, the most detailed mesh was selected for the NLFEA simulations.

<!-- side pdf=179 -->

##### **_12.6.2 Local response - mean material strength_**

A lower bound material strength in the pontoon will yield the largest damage to the pontoon, but there is some variability in the material strength within a generic material grade as defined in EUROCODE. The effect of using a mean strength rather than the lower bound values was investigated using the v6 pontoon model.

Variations of both the strength of the S420 and super duplex steel were included in the assessment. Some increase in the response can be found. As a pragmatic means to include this effect, a 10% increase in the maximum force-displacement from the lower-bound simulations were used to include the variability in material strength for the global response simulations.

##### **_12.6.3 Local response - upper bound material strength_**

The material standards give a wide range of material strengths within a material grade, with ultimate tensile strength of S420 steel ranging from 520 to 680 MPa. When ordering material in large batches from modern steel mills, one can typically ensure that the actual material does not exceed a certain strength. Hence, upper bound values of the tensile strength range from material standards are considered not relevant for the Bjørnafjorden project.

Upper-bound material strength was assessed as a sensitivity using the v9 pontoon model. Upperbound material strength was shown to have a significant effect on the force-displacement resistance of the pontoon, with increases up to 30%. However, even the upper-bound material strength of the ULS-optimized pontoon (v9) shows lower force vs. displacement response than the v6 pontoon with mean material strength as used in the Stage 3 global simulations. Hence, there is some robustness in the force-displacement relation used in the global simulation.

##### **_12.6.4 Local response - duplex material strength_**

There is a large discrepancy between the minimum material strength given in NORSOK M-630 [7] for the super duplex material strength and that from tests of the desired material grade. A sensitivity study was performed with the NORSOK minimum material strength, and a test conducted by Outokump on their 2507 material grade [8].

Changing the duplex material strength has a small effect on the response, much less than changing the impact location. There is some indication that a higher stiffness of the duplex material may yield earlier fracture, but not to the extent that the energy absorption is affected. Hence, varying duplex material properties were disregarded in the design loads.

##### **_12.6.5 Local response - high-ductile steels_**

The design basis specifies a minimum energy to be dissipated prior to fracture of the outer shell, as a measure to reduce consequences of the smaller impact events. This criterion has been challenging to meet without a significant reinforcement of the pontoon, which in turn increases the loads transferred from the pontoon to the column and bridge girder. An alternative means to meet the criterion is to use more ductile steels. This has been proposed by steel mills, shown in [9]. A. To investigate the possible gains of such materials, the S420 steel is replaced with the S420 HDS, and the super duplex 2507 with the more ductile 254 grade [10] with similar corrosive resistance as 2507.

A clear benefit was found, with delayed fracture and higher energy absorption pr. indentation. However, the cost of such materials is prohibitive for such a large construction project, and the ductile materials were not considered for design.

<!-- side pdf=180 -->

##### **_12.6.6 Local response - strain-rate sensitivity_**

The effect of strain-rate sensitivity on the work hardening of the material is often disregarded for ship impact events, in part due to the low strain rates (in the range of 1/s for the most strained elements) and in part due to the challenging nature of the strain-rate and lack of a full methodology to include the effect on both hardening and fracture.

A sensitivity study was conducted in two steps: First, calibrate a critical equivalent plastic strain level to give approximately the same force-displacement relation as with the BWH fracture criterion. Second, add strain-rate hardening to the simpler criterion to assess the change in response by adding strain-rate hardening. The strain-rate hardening effect was calibrated to the experiments in [11] for a S355 steel.

Some minor variations to the force level were observed when adding strain-rate effects, and the strain-rate sensitive simulations may indicate a somewhat earlier fracture. The overall response has however not changed drastically, including the peak load transferred further upwards in the bridge. Hence, the strain-rate hardening effect was neglected for the local collision simulations.

##### **_12.6.7 Local response - vertical impact position_**

The vertical impact location on the pontoon will vary depending on the vessel design, it’s loading condition and the environmental conditions at the time of impact. For the impact simulations used for global and local design, it was assumed that each vessel was sailing at its design draught. Several vertical impact locations were assessed to investigate the sensitivity to this assumption.

Position 1 and 3 from figure Figure 12-2 was chosen for this study, and impact locations 1m above and below the fully loaded impact location were checked Some variations can be found, but not larger than those from changing the horizontal impact location. The overall deformation mode of the pontoon remained unaffected.

##### **_12.6.8 Global response - increased impact energy_**

A doubling of the 10 000-year impact was considered to understand how and where response changes arise. The bridge response is sensitive to changing energy, but the sensitivity various along the structure.

Weak-axis moments in the bridge girder increase with increasing energy, especially in the higher parts of the floating bridge. Strong-axis moments increase with increasing energies, and the increase is greatest at the tower (A2) and towards the northern abutment (A40).

Column bending moments increase with increasing energy in a similar way for the weak-axis moment in the bridge girder, with a high sensitivity in the highest columns and lower for the lowest columns. Torsional moments show increased variation along the bridge, indicating that the modal response of the higher columns change for the higher impact energies whereas the lower columns are less affected.

Peak indentation to the pontoon increases by about 50% for a 100% increase in impact energy, but the ratio of local to global energy dissipation is less affected.

##### **_12.6.9 Global response - change in velocity_**

The design basis specifies a fixed relation between ship size (mass) and velocity to arrive at the target impact energy. However, as a ship impact is an impulse load, the relation between the mass and velocity will for a fixed level of energy still affect the structural response of the bridge girder. A

<!-- side pdf=181 -->

sensitivity study was conducted in which the impact speed varied from 5.1 m/s to 6 and 4 m/s respectively, and the striking mass adjusted so that the impact energy remained constant.

For the bridge girder, the weak-axis response is slightly increased with increasing velocity between axis A10 and A22, but the opposite is seen for the highest columns. For the strong-axis moment, a lower velocity gives higher response as the bridge has more time to deflect due to the slower buildup of force.

For the columns, bending moments around the transverse axis behave similarly as weak-axis bending moments. For bending moment around the longitudinal axis of the bridge, a higher velocity gives a slightly higher moment.

Overall, the effect of change in response due to change in velocity is minor but can be important for some structural elements if the reserve capacity to overloading is very small. It is recommended to further evaluate this effect during detail design if the reserve capacity is to be optimized.

#### **13.1 Fabrication #C1**

The fabrication of the bridge girders is assumed to be 126.8:3 = 42.267m long for the floating bridge and 24m for the cable stayed bridge and is assumed to be, as well as pontoons and steel columns, produced at fabrication yards typically outside Norway. All elements will be transported to assembly site at Hanøytangen (for the floating part) of the bridge site (for the cable stayed bridge).

The focus with regards to fabrication has been suitability for simple fabrication.

Pre-fabrication generally comprises fabrication of stiffeners and panels (i.e. for bridge girder, welding of longitudinal stiffeners on to skin plates). Trapezoidal stiffeners (U-ribs) are fabricated from steel plates and bent to the specified shape. The ends of the deck U-ribs must be beveled. The bulbprofiles need no further yard preparations.

The longitudinal skin plates for the general bridge section are in general subdivided into plates with different thicknesses where applicable. Furthermore, the bridge cross-section needs to be subdivided into manageable sizes for panel production and subsequent assembly. In the longitudinal direction a production length of 12.0m is common. It also complies with the need for this length to be multiple of the transverse truss spacing of 4.0 m.

In transverse direction, widths will typically be in the range 2.5 m to 3.5 m. This is governed by available sizes from the mill but also handling during fabrication and assembly. The location of panel joints is generally up to the fabricator to choose. The exception to this rule is in the deck structure where direct contact with wheel loads from traffic should be avoided.

By this approach, the transverse chords T-beams are also fabricated before panel assembly takes place. This involves cutting webs and flanges in lengths corresponding to the plate widths and making web cut-outs for longitudinal bulbs. The gusset plates for truss diagonals and verticals are also cut and welded on to the flanges (alternatively, for easier intermediate storage by stacking of panels, these items may be welded on just prior to assembly).

#### **13.9 Connection of super-elements in Søreidsvika #C9**

In Søreidsvika the super-elements are connected using temporary pin-connections in a weather window. With the pin-connections installed the structure can withstand a 10-year storm. In a new weather window the segment joint can be welded. When the segment is fully welded a new superelement can be connected.

The operations of connecting and welding require weather windows. Before connection is initiated preparation of the mooring shall be carried out. Within a weather window of 2-3 days the sections are connected, the pin-connections on the top plate are established and the mooring is tightened (Step 3-6). Within a new weather window of 2-3 days the segment joint is lined up and clamped. Thereafter welding of skin plates and throughs are finalized (Step 7).

The steps are described in detail below:

##### **Step 1 – Ballasting**

If required, floating bridge pontoons are ballasted to align the bridge end. The ballasting will rotate the bridge end and should only be done if the bridge ends are twisted out of tolerance.

##### **Step 2 – Mooring preparation**

Hook up and connection to preinstalled mooring lines are without tightening.

##### **Step 3 – Positioning**

The new super-element is towed into position.

##### **Step 4 – Winch system**

A winch system is attached on the bridge deck for pulling the segments together. The system is engaged when the segments are approximately 15 m apart. The winch system is designed to guide the segments both longitudinally and transversally.

##### **Step 5 – Pin-connection**

When the deck segments are aligned, strands in the pin-connection are installed and tensioned, connecting the main side with the support side.

The pin-connections shall be installed during deck fabrication, at the trial assembly, to secure the correct aligning of the segments during connection on site. The pin shall be horizontal in the deformed state where the end of the deck segment is cantilevered approximately 60 m.

##### **Step 6 – Re-stressing of the mooring**

When the pin-connection is installed, and the tightening of the mooring is finalized, the bridge is ready to withstand a 10-year storm with the hinged pin-connection.

##### **Step 7 – Welding**

Below the connection a barge supporting jacking towers is installed prior to the weather window. In a weather window of 2-3 days, the jacking towers on the barge are used to move up the bridge ends to align the segments and the segments are welded.

When the bridge ends are aligned the following steps shall be carried out:

- Strands in the pin-connection are de-tensioned.

- Deck segments are jacked apart.

- Shim plates are removed.

<!-- side pdf=204 -->

- For closing of the top plate - strands are re-tensioned.

- For closing of the bottom plate - tension ties are installed and tensioned.

- Closing shall be carried out until contact between the segments is obtained through the compression fitters to secure the correct welding gap.

- Overall geometry of the bridge shall be monitored and mitigated (if required)

- When contact is obtained through the compression fitters, plate alignment is trimmed and strongbacks are installed.

- Segments are welded.

- Jacking towers are dismantled.

It is estimated to take 12 hours to prepare for welding and sufficient welding will be performed within 24 hours in order to take a potential 10-year storm.

#### **13.12 Final installation of floating bridge #C12 to #C15**

The floating bridge will be towed from the holding position to site and connected at both ends of the bridge, in one operation. At the south end the floating bridge is connected to a cable stayed bridge cantilevering 400m into the fjord and at the north shore the floating bridge is connected to the already installed part of the bridge called the catamaran.

Before the final installation is commenced geometry control of the installed catamaran, the cable stayed bridge and the floating bridge in Søreidsvika shall be carried out to ensure that the tolerances are not exceeded. If the installation tolerances are exceeded resulting in a relative rotation of the segment ends, this shall be compensated by cutting each end of the floating bridge whilst still anchored in Søreidsvika. This is done to avoid build-in stresses during installation.

If the geometry control shows a relative twist between the segment ends, ballasting shall be used as a counter measure to ensure that the faces and the segment ends are within tolerances of the guiding system.

Within a weather window of 2-3 days the below steps 1-6 are carried out:

##### **Step 1 – Towing #C12**

The floating bridge is towed from the holding position to a position 100 m from final position. The bridge is hold in position only by tugboats.

_Figure 13-26 Tow from the holding position to a position 100 m from final position_

##### **Step 2 – Shortening of the bridge #C13**

The first and the last of the floating bridge pontoons (axis 3 and axis 37) is connected with a rope (Dyneema or similar) and the bridge is shortened by tensioning the rope by two clump weight of 100 tons. The bridge girder is shortned by approximately 30 m giving a clearance of 15 m at each end during installation. To ensure that the rope will not interfere with the tugboats operating, the rope is placed at 100 m depth.

<!-- side pdf=209 -->

Prior to moving from the holding position the shortening rope will be attached to the outer bridge pontoons, this is done outside the weather window. Small weights in the order of 5-10 tonnes will be attached to the tri-plates, such that the rope remains submerged but does not influence the dynamics of the bridge.

Once at location the 100-tonne clump weights will be attached to the tri plates which will induce the shortening action of the rope.

_Figure 13-27 Shortening of the bridge using Dynema submerged rope with heavy weights._

By shortening the bridge an infill-piece can be avoided. The use of an infill-piece is not suitable for the final installation of the floating bridge because of the limited weather window.

##### **Step 3 – Positioning #C13**

The shortened bridge is moved into the bridge axis using the tugboats. The shortening of the bridge gives an approximately 15 m free gap at both the north and the south end of the bridge.

##### **Step 4 – Winch system #C13**

When the bridge is in position winches are installed. The winches are in combination with the tugboats used to restrain the bridge position and straighten the bridge to close the gap between the bridge ends. The winching force is approximately 1300 kN at each end.

_Figure 13-28 Winch system_

##### **Step 5 – Guide system #C13**

A course guide system is installed at both sides of the deck segment which will guide the floating bridge into position within the tolerances of the pin connection. The guide system is active within the last 1.5 m and will cater for both horizontal and vertical tolerances and movements. The course guide system consists of a pin approximately 400 mm in diameter, a cone-shaped counterpart and a number of jacks allowing the system to be disconnected when the pin-connections are installed. The applied jacks are coupled with an accumulation tank with a gas expansion unit which will give a controlled force in the jacks when they are exposed for impacts.

##### **Step 8 – Welding #C15**

Before the weather restricted welding operation, a barge with jacking towers will be installed below the North connection. In a weather window of 2-3 days, the jacking towers on the barge are used to push up the north bridge ends to an aligned position, the south end are installed in a horizontal position due to the cable stayed bridge. The segments are welded simultaneously at both the north and the south end in the same weather window.

When the bridge ends are aligned clamping is carried out and the segments are welded. The welding operation, step 8 can be divided into the following subsections:

• 8.1 - Activation of jacking towers

<!-- side pdf=211 -->

Before the weather restricted welding operation, a barge with jacking towers will be installed below the North connection, retrained between the pontoons at axis 37 and 38. At the start of the weather window the jacking towers are used to move the bridge end up, aligning the segments.

- 8.2-8.5 - Closing of gap

In order to close the gap between the segments, the strands in the pin-connection shall be detensioned, the deck segments shall be jacked apart and shim plates shall be removed. When the shim plates are removed closing of the gap shall be carried out by re-tensioning the strands in the pinconnection. In addition to the strands in the pin connection, tension ties are installed and tensioned.

Closing shall be carried out until contact between the segments is obtained through the compression fitters to secure the correct welding gap.

- 8.6 - Plate alignment

When contact is obtained through the compression fitters, plate alignment is trimmed using aluminium strongbacks.

- 8.7 - Installation of steel strongbacks

When the joint is aligned steel strongbacks are installed. Steel strongbacks both ensure that plate surfaces remain aligned and counter transverse weld shrinkage, which otherwise could accumulate along weld joints. Weld induced angular deformation across the joints is also strongly reduced.

When welding of the joint is initiated 6-8 welders will be working simultaneously. Welding will be carried out continuously but can be divided as described in the following.

Prior to welding, grinding of the bevel shall be carried, down to bright and clean metal. This should be done while previous steps are carried out to minimize activities on the critical path.

- 8.8 - Installation of backing

Built-in adhesive tape combined with magnetic supports is typically used to support the ceramic backing for the circumferential skin plate joint.

- 8.9 - Circumferential butt joint (phase 1)

Welding at the easily accessible areas between the compression fitters will be carried out first. Two weld passes will be performed (root pass and first fill pass), resulting in a weld thickness of minimum 8 mm.

- 8.10 - Removal of the tension ties and compression fitters

When welding at the locations described in step 8.9 is finalized, removal of the tension ties and cutting of the compression fitters are commenced.

- 8.11 - Circumferential butt joint (phase 2)

Welding of the areas at the tension ties. Two weld passes will be performed (root pass and first fill pass), resulting in a weld thickness of minimum 8 mm.

Simultaneously as the welding is carried out, the pin-connection shall be removed. Plates welded to the deck plate shall be removed to make room for welding. Final repair shall be carried out outside the weather window.

- 8.12 - Circumferential butt joint (phase 3)

<!-- side pdf=212 -->

Finalizing of the full circumferential two weld passes of the skin plates by welding of the top plate at the location of the pin-connection. Two weld passes will be performed (root pass and first fill pass), resulting in a weld thickness of minimum 8 mm.

- 8.13 - Circumferential butt joint (phase 4)

Finalizing of the skin plate welding to full thickness. 6-8 welders will weld simultaneously to finalize the skin plate welding.

- 8.14 - Non-destructive testing (NDT)

NDT and repair of weld defects of circumferential joints must be performed before trough splices are installed.

- 8.15 – Repair of weld defects and preparation for welding of stiffener infill pieces

The defects found with NDT shall be repaired and new NDT shall be carried out at the affected area. Simultaneously as the repair of weld defects is carried out weld reinforcement at stiffener locations shall be ground away.

Length of infill pieces will be checked during the trial assembly so no adjustment will be expected.

- 8.16 - Stiffener Installation

Firstly, the stiffeners are installed and tack welded to keep in place. After the stiffeners are in place the welding can be carried out.

- 8.17 - Non-destructive testing (NDT)

NDT and repair of weld defects of the infill pieces shall be carried out.

- 8.18 - Jacking towers dismantling

When welding is finalized the jacking towers are dismantled.

- 8.19 - Removal of temporary structures and surface repair

Removal of temporary structures and surface repair will be carried out outside the weather window.

#### **13.13 Mooring lines hook-up #C14**

After the connection of the floating bridge in both ends, the mooring lines will be hooked up to the pontoons from wet storage and tensioned up.

The hookup of the bridge to the mooring system consists of the steps outlined below. The mooring lines are pre-installed and laid down on the seabed, and the transfer messenger lines for fairleads are pre-installed on pontoons. All steps are performed in tandem. i.e. one vessel on the west side and one vessel on the east side of the bridge:

- Retrieve wet stored mooring line.

- Back AHTS towards pontoon and transfer fairlead messenger line to vessel.

- Engage fendering between pontoon and AHTS.

- Pull chain through fairlead with help of messenger line.

- (If not already performed during installation of lines) pull to break-out load of inverse catenary (approx 600t), for details see associated Appendix M.

- Release load until visually marked chain link is at chain stopper.

- Lock chain stopper.

- Release chain from vessel.

- Deballast pontoon to obtain desired draught.t

- Verify desired tension in fairlead at correct pontoon draught (fairlead is designed with tensionsensor – see Appendix M).

The hook-up and tensioning of mooring system may be time-critical in order to maintain the installation schedule of the bridge. However, the installation may be performed in quite rough wave conditions (Hs<1m), and is mainly dependent on the relative motion between the pontoon and vessel – assumed to be very low in the sheltered conditions in Bjørnafjorden. As such the workability to perform this operation should be high and is assumed to be performed prior to the more restricted weather window for the welding phase.

The installation vessel picks up the mooring line from the seafloor and pulls the mooring line through the chain stopper on the pontoon and pre-tensions the mooring line to the defined pre-tension.

#### **14.1 Operation and maintenance driven BIM requirements for future phases**

#### **14.2 Material selection and corrosion protection – Pontoons and splash zone**

<mark>One of the primary goals for the project is to reduce the scope for inspection, maintenance, and repair during the lifetime. The purpose of one of the reports enclosed in Appendix O is to advise a material selection and corrosion protection design for the bridge pontoons that balances investment cost, operational cost, and risk. First, a coating selection matrix and a typical coating maintenance schedule is proposed. It has been highlighted that coating friendly design is an important factor which has a strong influence on the useful lifetime of a coating system. As such, the design of the pontoons - external surfaces in particular - should be kept as simple and smooth as possible. Certain outfitting details will, however, be required to facilitate construction and marine operations (hatches, mooring/towing aids, etc.). Details for temporary operations are normally not designed with optimal O&M in mind. This is seen as a risk for O&M since coating applied in the field will be of lesser quality than coating applied in shop. The design of details for temporary operations should be based on a multidiscipline approach to ensure that the details do not trigger unnecessary O&M. This can be performed when details for construction and marine operation have matured.</mark> <u><mark>Second,</mark></u> <mark>preliminary cathodic protection calculations to establish anode weights for the external and internal parts of the pontoons have been included. Third, a belt of a corrosion resistant alloy as the outer skin of the pontoons in the splash zone was introduced in an earlier phase of the project. For this phase it has been decided that the belt shall extend from 1.0 m below the lowest waterline and all the way up the vertical side of the pontoons, i.e. approx. 4.5 m in height. Three alternatives have been evaluated; a) solid 25Cr SDSS, b) roll bonded CS with alloy 625 cladding, and c) loose cladding in 25Cr SDSS. A comparison of material and welding cost is presented. This shows that roll bonded plates with alloy 625 cladding is prohibitively expensive. The alternative with loose cladding in 25Cr SDSS involves significant qualification work both from design, fabrication method and welding point of view. For these reasons, solid 25Cr SDSS is maintained as base case. Fourth, a list of recommendations for further work has been made, including evaluation of a new and cost effective alternative cladding material for roll bonded plates.</mark>

## **15 Geology**

Appendix V is a summary of previous memos and reports from several field investigations regarding the geological conditions at both sides of the Bjørnafjorden bridge. A site visit has been performed. A more detailed presentation of e.g. petrography and rock mass jointing is found in Appendix V, Enclosure 1.

Main focus on the investigations has been on the conditions at Svarvhelleholmen which will be the site for the bridge tower as pictured on the cover page. The overall impression at this site is that the rock mass is a rather homogeneous conglomerate and well suited for location of the tower foundations: No unfavorable joints have been identified that may lead to global instability towards the sea. The rock has high strength and E-modulus, which indicate that there will be a negligible vertical deformation under the tower foundations.

The rock type on land, at Reksteren south of Svarvhelleholmen, is a massive and hard granodiorite with apparently a scattered joint pattern. There are few rock outcrops in the area because of a relatively thin cover of peat bog.

The bedrock at Gulholmen at the northern abutment is more heterogeneous, with two main rock units: The western half of the island is dominated by a quartz diorite (Trondhjemitt) and the eastern part is dominated by metavolcanic rock types like metatuffites and metamorphosed lavas. This sequence can be denominated as amphibolite. Both rock units are regarded as “hardrock” with more or less the same mechanical properties. The footprint of the abutment will be blasted down to an even level, and the abutment forces are assumed to be taken by shear capacity between the abutment bottom and blasted rock surface.
